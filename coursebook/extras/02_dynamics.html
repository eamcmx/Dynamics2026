Lecture 02 · Module 2 · Robot Manipulator Dynamics

Energy-Based Approach to Robot Dynamics

Lagrangian mechanics: from kinetic and potential energy to a systematic dynamic model.

Lecturer. bibliosense Lecture. 02
Force methods get you through a pendulum, but they collapse the moment you stack three links and a few constraint forces on top of each other. So we switch tools. In this module we build the dynamics out of energy — kinetic and potential — and let the Lagrangian and the Euler–Lagrange equations do the bookkeeping. By the end you'll have the standard manipulator equation $\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})\ddot{\mathbf{q}} + \mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}}) + \mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q}) = \boldsymbol{\tau}$ and, more importantly, you'll understand where every term comes from.

0 What this lecture covers

This module reframes robot dynamics around energy instead of forces. We start with the practical question — why bother with Lagrangian mechanics when Newton–Euler already works? — and then build up the machinery: kinetic and potential energy in multi-link systems, generalized coordinates, the Lagrangian $L = T - V$, and the Euler–Lagrange equations. From there we walk the systematic derivation that produces the standard manipulator equation $\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})\ddot{\mathbf{q}} + \mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}}) + \mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q}) = \boldsymbol{\tau}$, and we close with a 2-DOF self-assessment that you'll carry into Module 3.

If Module 1 taught you to see forces on a single body, Module 2 teaches you to count energy across a chain of them — and let the calculus of variations handle the constraints for free.
Learning objectives. Master kinetic/potential energy in multi-link systems, the Lagrangian framework, generalized coordinates, the Euler–Lagrange equations, and how all of this systematically produces the manipulator equation.

1 Why energy methods beat force methods

Force-based and energy-based dynamics are mathematically equivalent — they yield the same equations of motion. The difference is the path to those equations. For a single pendulum, drawing a free-body diagram and applying $\mathbf{F} = m\mathbf{a}$ is the most intuitive thing in the world. For a 6-DOF arm with revolute joints, that approach turns into a forest of constraint forces, vector transformations, and coupling terms that are very easy to get wrong.

The central advantage of the energy approach is that it operates on scalar quantities. Kinetic energy $T$ and potential energy $V$ are scalars; they don't care about coordinate frames in the way force vectors do. Constraint forces — the reaction loads at every joint — automatically drop out of the final equations because they do no work along the allowed motion. What remains is a systematic, almost mechanical procedure that scales gracefully with degrees of freedom.

Newton–Euler is a scalpel; Lagrangian mechanics is a CNC mill. For a single cut, the scalpel wins. For an arm full of joints, you want the mill.
Force-based (Newton–Euler). Free-body diagram per link, force/moment balance, explicit constraint forces, careful frame transformations. Powerful but error-prone as DOF grows.
Energy-based (Lagrangian). Compute scalar $T$ and $V$, form $L = T - V$, apply the Euler–Lagrange equations. Constraint forces vanish. The procedure is the same for any robot.
3-DOF arm — side by side. Force method: 3 free-body diagrams, 6 balance equations, constraint elimination. Energy method: 3 KE expressions + 3 PE expressions → form $L$ → apply Euler–Lagrange. Same answer; far less ceremony.

2 Kinetic energy in multi-link systems

Kinetic energy in robotics is more involved than the textbook $\tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$. Every link has both a translational component (motion of its center of mass) and a rotational component (rotation about the COM), and the velocities of every link are coupled through the kinematic chain. When joint 1 moves, every downstream link moves — each with a velocity that depends on the motion of all preceding joints.

For a robot with $n$ links, the total kinetic energy is

$$T = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \left[ m_i \mathbf{v}_i^T \mathbf{v}_i + \boldsymbol{\omega}_i^T \mathbf{I}_i \boldsymbol{\omega}_i \right]$$

where $m_i$ is the mass of link $i$, $\mathbf{v}_i$ is the velocity of its center of mass, $\boldsymbol{\omega}_i$ is its angular velocity, and $\mathbf{I}_i$ is its inertia tensor about the COM. The crucial point: both $\mathbf{v}_i$ and $\boldsymbol{\omega}_i$ depend on the joint velocities of every joint between the base and link $i$ — that's where the coupling terms come from.

The translational and rotational pieces are the easy part. The hard part is that each $\mathbf{v}_i$ is a chain-rule cascade of every joint upstream of it.
Translation vs. rotation. Each link generally contributes both a translational COM term and a rotational term about the COM. Both must appear in $T$.
Velocity coupling. The velocity of any point on link $i$ depends on $\dot{q}_1, \ldots, \dot{q}_i$. This dependency is what makes robot dynamics nonlinear and coupled.

3 Potential energy and configuration

Potential energy in robotics is almost always gravitational, and unlike kinetic energy it depends only on configuration — not velocity. For a robot in a uniform gravitational field,

$$V = \sum_{i=1}^{n} m_i \mathbf{g}^T \mathbf{r}_i$$

where $\mathbf{g}$ is the gravity vector (typically $[0, 0, -9.81]^T$ m/s$^2$) and $\mathbf{r}_i$ is the position of the COM of link $i$.

The consequence: $V$ changes dramatically with posture. A horizontally-extended arm sits at much higher $V$ than the same arm folded vertically — and the difference shows up in the joint torques as the gravity loading the actuators must hold against. Choose a sensible reference height and the algebra cleans up; choose a bad one and you'll carry constants you don't need.

PE is the easy half of the Lagrangian — it's just heights. The trick is picking the reference frame that makes the heights cheap to write down.
Reference frame matters. PE only ever appears through derivatives, so the absolute reference is arbitrary — but a clever choice removes constant terms and simplifies the algebra.
Configuration dependence. $V(\mathbf{q})$ is what produces the gravitational torque vector $\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q})$ in the final manipulator equation.

4 The Lagrangian and generalized coordinates

Lagrangian mechanics — Joseph-Louis Lagrange, 18th century — is the framework that lets us derive equations of motion directly from energy. Two ideas drive it: generalized coordinates and the Lagrangian function.

Generalized coordinates are any independent set of parameters that fully specifies the configuration. For robots the natural choice is the joint variables themselves: revolute angles and prismatic displacements. A robot with $n$ joints has $n$ DOF, and the joint vector $\mathbf{q} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ gives a minimal description — kinematic constraints are satisfied automatically by the choice of coordinates, and each $q_i$ corresponds directly to an actuator.

The Lagrangian itself is

$$L(\mathbf{q}, \dot{\mathbf{q}}, t) = T(\mathbf{q}, \dot{\mathbf{q}}) - V(\mathbf{q})$$

It's a single scalar — the excess of kinetic over potential energy. The principle of stationary action says physical trajectories are exactly those that extremize the time integral $\int L\,dt$. That's where the equations of motion come from.

You spend most of the work building $T$ and $V$. Once you have $L$, the rest is a recipe.
Why joint variables are perfect. They're minimal (no redundant coordinates), they automatically respect kinematic constraints, and they map one-to-one to physical actuators — joint $i$'s torque drives joint $i$'s coordinate.
The Lagrangian
L(q, q̇, t) = T(q, q̇) - V(q)

  q  : generalized coordinates  (joint variables)
  q̇  : generalized velocities
  T  : total kinetic energy
  V  : total potential energy

5 The Euler–Lagrange equations

The Euler–Lagrange equations are the systematic procedure for turning $L$ into equations of motion. For a system with $n$ DOF,

$$\frac{d}{dt}\!\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot{q}_i}\right) - \frac{\partial L}{\partial q_i} = \tau_i, \qquad i = 1, 2, \ldots, n$$

where $\tau_i$ is the generalized force conjugate to coordinate $q_i$ — for revolute joints, that's a torque; for prismatic joints, a linear force. The derivation comes out of calculus of variations, but you don't need to redo that work each time. The recipe is fixed:

  1. Express $T$ and $V$ in terms of $\mathbf{q}$ and $\dot{\mathbf{q}}$.
  2. Form $L = T - V$.
  3. Compute the partial derivatives.
  4. Substitute into the Euler–Lagrange equation for each $i$.
  5. Simplify.

That's it. The same five steps work for a 1-DOF pendulum and a 7-DOF redundant arm.

Once you trust the recipe, the Euler–Lagrange equations stop feeling like math and start feeling like an algorithm — one you can hand to a symbolic solver.
Generalized force $\tau_i$. For revolute joints this is the joint torque; for prismatic joints, the joint force. Constraint forces never appear — they were eliminated by the choice of generalized coordinates.
Worked walk-through: 2-DOF planar arm. Coordinates $q_1 = \theta_1$, $q_2 = \theta_2$. Link 1 has simple rotation about the base; link 2 couples to link 1 through both position and angular velocity. Even this minimal example produces coupled, nonlinear equations with inertial coupling, Coriolis/centrifugal terms, and gravitational loading — exactly the structure we'll see in the general manipulator equation.
Euler–Lagrange equations
d/dt ( ∂L/∂q̇ᵢ ) - ∂L/∂qᵢ = τᵢ      for i = 1, …, n

Recipe:
  1. Write T(q, q̇) and V(q).
  2. L = T - V.
  3. Compute ∂L/∂q̇ᵢ  and  ∂L/∂qᵢ.
  4. Take d/dt of ∂L/∂q̇ᵢ.
  5. Substitute and simplify.

6 Velocity kinematics for energy calculations

The hardest practical step in applying Lagrangian mechanics to robots is getting the velocities right. Link velocities are not independent — they're coupled through the kinematic chain — and the kinetic energy expression is only as accurate as those velocities.

Linear velocity. If the COM of link $i$ sits at $\mathbf{r}_i(\mathbf{q})$, then by the chain rule

$$\mathbf{v}_i = \frac{\partial \mathbf{r}_i}{\partial q_1}\dot{q}_1 + \cdots + \frac{\partial \mathbf{r}_i}{\partial q_i}\dot{q}_i = \mathbf{J}_{vi}(\mathbf{q})\,\dot{\mathbf{q}}$$

where $\mathbf{J}_{vi}$ is the linear-velocity Jacobian for the COM of link $i$.

Angular velocity. For revolute joints, each upstream joint contributes its joint rate to the angular velocity of every link beyond it. For prismatic joints, no angular contribution. So in a planar 3-revolute chain, $\boldsymbol{\omega}_1 = \dot{q}_1$, $\boldsymbol{\omega}_2 = \dot{q}_1 + \dot{q}_2$, $\boldsymbol{\omega}_3 = \dot{q}_1 + \dot{q}_2 + \dot{q}_3$ — the deeper into the chain you go, the more joints you depend on, and the more coupling terms appear in $T$.

Almost every mistake in a Lagrangian derivation traces back to a velocity. Get the Jacobians right and the rest is bookkeeping.
Planar 3-revolute example. $\boldsymbol{\omega}_1 = \dot{q}_1$; $\boldsymbol{\omega}_2 = \dot{q}_1 + \dot{q}_2$; $\boldsymbol{\omega}_3 = \dot{q}_1 + \dot{q}_2 + \dot{q}_3$. Each subsequent link is touched by more joints — that's the source of the coupling in $T$.
Compact form. $\mathbf{v}_i = \mathbf{J}_{vi}(\mathbf{q})\,\dot{\mathbf{q}}$ — the COM velocity is linear in joint rates, with a configuration-dependent Jacobian.

7 Kinetic energy in matrix form: the inertia matrix

Once velocities are written in terms of joint rates, $T$ becomes a quadratic form in $\dot{\mathbf{q}}$:

$$T = \frac{1}{2}\,\dot{\mathbf{q}}^T \mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})\,\dot{\mathbf{q}}$$

where the inertia matrix $\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})$ has elements

$$D_{ij}(\mathbf{q}) = \sum_{k=1}^{n}\left[m_k\!\left(\frac{\partial \mathbf{r}_k}{\partial q_i}\right)^{\!T}\!\!\left(\frac{\partial \mathbf{r}_k}{\partial q_j}\right) + \left(\frac{\partial \boldsymbol{\omega}_k}{\partial q_i}\right)^{\!T}\!\!\mathbf{I}_k\!\left(\frac{\partial \boldsymbol{\omega}_k}{\partial q_j}\right)\right]$$

The diagonal entries $D_{ii}$ are the effective inertia seen by joint $i$ when only joint $i$ moves. The off-diagonal entries $D_{ij}$ encode inertial coupling — moving joint $j$ accelerates joint $i$ even with $\tau_i = 0$. And $\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})$ is configuration-dependent, which is what makes the dynamics genuinely nonlinear.

Two properties matter for everything that follows: $\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})$ is always symmetric and positive definite. Symmetry comes from the structure of the kinetic energy; positive definiteness reflects the fact that nonzero motion must cost positive kinetic energy. Together they guarantee the dynamics are physically consistent and mathematically well-posed.

The inertia matrix is where the geometry of the arm gets compiled into the dynamics. Once you have $\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})$, the rest of the manipulator equation falls out of derivatives.
$D_{ii}$ — diagonal. Effective inertia at joint $i$ when only joint $i$ moves. Always strictly positive.
$D_{ij}$ — off-diagonal. Inertial coupling between joints $i$ and $j$. Vanishes only in special configurations or for decoupled designs.
Symmetric and positive-definite. $\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q}) = \mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})^T \succ 0$ for all reachable $\mathbf{q}$. This is what guarantees the dynamics are well-posed.
Kinetic energy as a quadratic form
T(q, q̇) = ½ q̇ᵀ D(q) q̇

  D(q)  : n×n inertia matrix  (symmetric, positive-definite)
  Dᵢᵢ  : effective inertia of joint i
  Dᵢⱼ  : inertial coupling between joints i and j

8 From Lagrangian to the manipulator equation

Now we run the recipe end-to-end. Starting from $T = \tfrac{1}{2}\dot{\mathbf{q}}^T\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})\dot{\mathbf{q}}$ and $V(\mathbf{q})$, the Euler–Lagrange equations produce the standard manipulator equation:

$$\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})\,\ddot{\mathbf{q}} + \mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}}) + \mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q}) = \boldsymbol{\tau}$$

Three physically meaningful pieces fall out cleanly:

  • Inertia $\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})$ — straight from $T$. Symmetric, positive definite, configuration-dependent.
  • Coriolis/centrifugal $\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}})$ — velocity-dependent forces from the time derivative of $\mathbf{D}$. Component-wise, $h_i = \sum_{j,k} h_{ijk}\,\dot{q}_j\dot{q}_k$ with the Christoffel symbols $h_{ijk} = \tfrac{1}{2}\!\left(\partial D_{ij}/\partial q_k + \partial D_{ik}/\partial q_j - \partial D_{jk}/\partial q_i\right)$.
  • Gravity $\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q})$ — straight from $V$, with $c_i = \partial V/\partial q_i$.

That's the entire structure of robot dynamics. Same procedure, every robot. The same form is what real-time controllers evaluate at $\geq 1$ kHz inside production manipulators.

Three terms, three physical meanings. Once you've seen this decomposition, every manipulator equation in the textbook stops looking like a wall of symbols.
$\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})$ — inertia. From $T$. Symmetric, positive-definite. Captures effective inertia and inertial coupling.
$\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}})$ — Coriolis & centrifugal. From $\dot{\mathbf{D}}$. Velocity-quadratic, captures motion-coupling effects. Built from the Christoffel symbols of $\mathbf{D}$.
$\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q})$ — gravity. From $V$. $c_i = \partial V/\partial q_i$. The torques the actuators must supply just to hold the arm still.
Step 1 — kinetic-energy derivatives
∂T/∂q̇ᵢ = Σⱼ Dᵢⱼ(q) q̇ⱼ

d/dt ( ∂T/∂q̇ᵢ ) = Σⱼ Dᵢⱼ(q) q̈ⱼ
                 + Σⱼ Σₖ (∂Dᵢⱼ/∂qₖ) q̇ₖ q̇ⱼ
Step 2 — position derivatives
∂T/∂qᵢ = ½ Σⱼ Σₖ (∂Dⱼₖ/∂qᵢ) q̇ⱼ q̇ₖ
∂V/∂qᵢ = gravitational term for joint i
Step 3 — Euler–Lagrange substitution
d/dt(∂L/∂q̇ᵢ) - ∂L/∂qᵢ = τᵢ
  ⇒  Σⱼ Dᵢⱼ(q) q̈ⱼ + hᵢ(q, q̇) + cᵢ(q) = τᵢ
Step 4 — manipulator equation
D(q) q̈ + h(q, q̇) + c(q) = τ

  D(q)      : inertia matrix
  h(q, q̇)  : Coriolis / centrifugal
  c(q)      : gravity
Christoffel symbols of the first kind
hᵢⱼₖ = ½ ( ∂Dᵢⱼ/∂qₖ + ∂Dᵢₖ/∂qⱼ - ∂Dⱼₖ/∂qᵢ )

hᵢ(q, q̇) = Σⱼ Σₖ hᵢⱼₖ q̇ⱼ q̇ₖ

9 Why the energy-based approach wins in practice

Stepping back: what did Lagrangian mechanics actually buy us? Five things, and they compound.

  • Systematic procedure. The same five steps generate the equations for any robot — 1-DOF or 7-DOF, planar or spatial, revolute or prismatic.
  • Physical insight. Each term in the manipulator equation has a clear meaning: $\mathbf{D}$ is inertia, $\mathbf{h}$ is Coriolis/centrifugal, $\mathbf{c}$ is gravity. You can read off what each does to the arm.
  • Computational structure. The matrix form $\mathbf{D}\ddot{\mathbf{q}} + \mathbf{h} + \mathbf{c} = \boldsymbol{\tau}$ is what real-time controllers evaluate at high rate.
  • Error reduction. No constraint forces, no sign errors from frame transforms, no missing coupling terms.
  • Automation. The whole procedure can be — and routinely is — implemented in symbolic computation packages like Maple, Mathematica, or sympy.

In industrial practice this is more than a derivation choice. Modern controllers compute $\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})$, $\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}})$, and $\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q})$ at $\geq 1$ kHz to drive trajectory tracking, optimize energy use, support collision detection, and predict mechanical wear. Robots running model-based controllers built on this framework typically hit sub-millimeter accuracy at high speed, with significant cycle-time and energy improvements over plain PID control.

Newton–Euler will get you the dynamics. Lagrangian mechanics will get you the dynamics and a structure you can build a controller on top of.
Industry footprint. Embedded robot controllers evaluate $\mathbf{D}$, $\mathbf{h}$, $\mathbf{c}$ at 1 kHz or faster — for trajectory optimization, energy management, collision detection, and predictive maintenance.
