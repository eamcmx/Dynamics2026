Lecture 05 · Vector Algebra

Vectors — definitions, geometry & linear operations

From an ordered triplet of numbers to a directed segment we can add, scale, project, and decompose.

Lecturer. bibliosense Lecture. 05
We start with the simplest possible object that carries both a size and a direction: a vector. The trick is that one object lives in two worlds at once — a triplet of numbers in some coordinate system, and a directed line segment you can draw on paper. Today we set up the algebra (addition, scaling, subtraction), the geometry (translation, projection), and the rectangular basis $\mathbf{i}, \mathbf{j}, \mathbf{k}$ that lets us pass freely between the two.

0 What this lecture covers

Two views of the same object — a triplet $\{a_1,a_2,a_3\}$ and an arrow in space — and the entire lecture is the bridge between them.

We introduce vectors as ordered triplets of numbers in a coordinate system, equipped with rules of addition and multiplication by a scalar, and transforming under rotations exactly as the coordinates of a point do.

From there we move to the geometric picture: a vector as the directed segment $\overrightarrow{P_1P_2}$, its length $|\mathbf{a}|$, the unit vector $\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{a}/a$, and the projection of one vector onto another. We close with the properties of linear operations and the first step toward decomposition into a rectangular orthogonal basis.

Notation. Vectors are written in boldface: $\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b}, \mathbf{c}$. The italic $a = |\mathbf{a}|$ is the magnitude — a scalar.

1 Basic definitions

The transformation rule under rotation is what separates a real vector from any old triplet of numbers.

A three-dimensional vector in a given coordinate system is an ordered triplet of numbers that obeys certain rules of addition and multiplication, and that transforms under rotations of the coordinate system just as the coordinates of a point do. The members of the triplet are the coordinates of the vector. The same idea generalises to $n$-dimensional vectors.

The notation $\mathbf{a} = \{a_1, a_2, a_3\}$ means that $a_1, a_2, a_3$ are the coordinates of $\mathbf{a}$ in 3-D. Two vectors are equal iff their coordinates are respectively equal: $\mathbf{a} = \mathbf{b} \iff a_1 = b_1,\; a_2 = b_2,\; a_3 = b_3.$ A single vector equality is therefore equivalent to a system of three scalar equalities.

Why the rotation rule matters. Many triplets of numbers (e.g. $\{m, T, V\}$ — mass, temperature, volume) are not vectors. The transformation rule is the actual definition.

2 Linear vector operations: scaling, sum, difference

Componentwise on the algebra side; tip-to-tail on the geometric side. Same object, two languages.

Scalar multiplication. If $\mathbf{b} = \lambda \mathbf{a}$, then each coordinate is scaled by $\lambda$: $b_1 = \lambda a_1,\quad b_2 = \lambda a_2,\quad b_3 = \lambda a_3.$

Addition. The sum of $\mathbf{a} = \{a_1, a_2, a_3\}$ and $\mathbf{b} = \{b_1, b_2, b_3\}$ is $\mathbf{c} = \mathbf{a} + \mathbf{b} = \{a_1+b_1,\; a_2+b_2,\; a_3+b_3\}.$

Difference. Subtraction is defined via addition of the opposite: $\mathbf{c} = \mathbf{a} - \mathbf{b} = \mathbf{a} + (-\mathbf{b}), \quad c_i = a_i - b_i.$

Geometric check. $\mathbf{c} = \mathbf{a} - \mathbf{b}$ is exactly the vector that satisfies $\mathbf{c} + \mathbf{b} = \mathbf{a}$ — i.e. the arrow from the head of $\mathbf{b}$ to the head of $\mathbf{a}$ when both share a tail.

3 Geometric interpretation in 3-D space

Pick any tail you like — by parallel translation, equal vectors slide on top of each other.

Place a rectangular coordinate system. Given $\mathbf{a} = \{a_1, a_2, a_3\}$ we can always choose two points $P_1(x_1,y_1,z_1)$ and $P_2(x_2,y_2,z_2)$ such that $a_1 = x_2 - x_1,\quad a_2 = y_2 - y_1,\quad a_3 = z_2 - z_1.$ Then $\mathbf{a}$ is interpreted as the directed line segment $\overrightarrow{P_1P_2}$. $P_1$ is the tail (base, origin); $P_2$ is the head.

The coordinates of $\overrightarrow{P_1P_2}$ are the differences of the coordinates of head and tail: $\overrightarrow{P_1P_2} = \{x_2-x_1,\; y_2-y_1,\; z_2-z_1\}.$

The length of $\mathbf{a}$ is the length of the segment $P_1P_2$. A vector carries magnitude and direction at once: $\mathbf{a}$ is the vector, $a = |\mathbf{a}|$ is its scalar magnitude.

If the tail sits at the origin and the head at $P(x,y,z)$, the vector is the radius-vector of $P$, written $\mathbf{r}$.

Free vs. bound. Because equal vectors coincide under parallel translation, a vector is essentially free — only its magnitude and direction matter, not where you draw it.

4 Linear operations, geometrically

Scaling stretches; negation flips; addition slides one tail onto the other's head.

Equality. By parallel translation, equal vectors coincide.

Scalar multiplication. $\mathbf{b} = \lambda \mathbf{a}$ has length $|\mathbf{b}| = |\lambda|\,a$. If $\lambda > 0$, $\mathbf{b}$ points the same way as $\mathbf{a}$; if $\lambda < 0$, $\mathbf{b}$ points the opposite way. The opposite of $\overrightarrow{AB}$ is $\overrightarrow{BA} = -\overrightarrow{AB}$.

Unit vector. A unit vector has length 1. For any non-zero $\mathbf{a}$, $\mathbf{u} = \dfrac{\mathbf{a}}{a}$ is the unit vector in the direction of $\mathbf{a}$.

Sum. Tip-to-tail: place the tail of $\mathbf{b}$ at the head of $\mathbf{a}$; the sum runs from the tail of $\mathbf{a}$ to the head of $\mathbf{b}$ (parallelogram rule).

Difference. $\mathbf{c} = \mathbf{a} - \mathbf{b} = \mathbf{a} + (-\mathbf{b})$, equivalently the unique $\mathbf{c}$ with $\mathbf{c} + \mathbf{b} = \mathbf{a}$.

Unit vectors. Dividing a vector by its own length always gives a direction-only object — handy for separating where it points from how big it is.

5 Projection of a vector in a given direction

The projection is a signed shadow: same direction is positive, opposite direction is negative.

Let $\theta$ be the angle between two vectors $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$. The projection of $\mathbf{a}$ onto $\mathbf{b}$ is the scalar $\operatorname{Proj}_{\mathbf{b}} \mathbf{a} = a \cos\theta. \qquad (1)$

  • If $\theta$ is acute, the projection is positive.
  • If $\theta$ is obtuse, the projection is negative.

If the chosen direction is the $x$-axis, then the projection of $\mathbf{a}$ onto the $x$-axis equals the difference between the $x$-coordinates of the endpoints of the vector — exactly the first coordinate of $\mathbf{a}$.

Sign matters. Forgetting that $\cos\theta < 0$ for obtuse $\theta$ is the most common projection mistake. The sign carries real geometric information.

6 Properties of linear vector operations

Vectors form a vector space — these three rules are the axioms doing the heavy lifting.

All of the following follow directly from the properties of real numbers applied componentwise — proofs are left as a short exercise.

1. Commutative law for addition: $\mathbf{a} + \mathbf{b} = \mathbf{b} + \mathbf{a}.$

2. Associative law for addition: $\mathbf{a} + (\mathbf{b} + \mathbf{c}) = (\mathbf{a} + \mathbf{b}) + \mathbf{c} = \mathbf{a} + \mathbf{b} + \mathbf{c}.$

3. Distributive laws for scalar multiplication over addition: $\lambda(\mathbf{a} + \mathbf{b}) = \lambda\mathbf{a} + \lambda\mathbf{b}, \qquad (\lambda + \mu)\,\mathbf{a} = \lambda\mathbf{a} + \mu\mathbf{a}.$

Try it. Pick any concrete $\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b},\mathbf{c}$ and any $\lambda,\mu \in \mathbb{R}$ and verify each law componentwise — that is the proof.

7 Decomposition: the rectangular orthogonal basis

A basis is just a minimal set of arrows you can mix to build everything else.

Let $\mathbf{i} = \{1, 0, 0\}$ be the unit vector in the positive direction of the $x$-axis. Any vector lying along the $x$-axis, $\mathbf{a} = \{a_x, 0, 0\}$, can be written as $\mathbf{a} = \{a_x, 0, 0\} = a_x\{1, 0, 0\} = a_x\,\mathbf{i}.$

The single vector $\mathbf{i}$ is a basis for the one-dimensional space of vectors along the $x$-axis: every such vector is one scalar multiple of $\mathbf{i}$.

This is the foundation for the full 3-D rectangular basis $\mathbf{i}, \mathbf{j}, \mathbf{k}$ — to be built up in the next section, decomposing an arbitrary $\mathbf{a}$ as $a_x\mathbf{i} + a_y\mathbf{j} + a_z\mathbf{k}$.

What's coming. Add $\mathbf{j} = \{0,1,0\}$ and $\mathbf{k} = \{0,0,1\}$ and you can write any 3-D vector as $\mathbf{a} = a_x\mathbf{i} + a_y\mathbf{j} + a_z\mathbf{k}$.
