Lecture 01 · Module 1 · Robot Dynamics

From kinematics to dynamics

Why forces and torques — not just geometry — decide whether a robot actually works.

Lecturer. bibliosense Lecture. 01
Up to now you've been geometers — you can tell me where the end-effector is, how fast it's moving, and which joint angles get it there. That's the easy half. Today we cross into dynamics, which is the messy, physical question: what does it actually take to make that motion happen? Forces, torques, inertias, gravity fighting back. This is the lecture that turns a kinematic stick-figure into a real machine you can build, size, and control.

0 What this lecture covers

Kinematics tells us what moves; dynamics tells us what causes the motion. In this opening module we make that transition explicit and set up the mathematical scaffolding for everything that follows.

We'll see why dynamics is non-negotiable for real robots, classify problems into forward and inverse dynamics, and meet the master equation $\boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})\ddot{\mathbf{q}} + \mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}}) + \mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q})$ that the rest of the course will dissect, derive, and put to work.

Treat this lecture as the map of the territory. Don't worry yet about deriving anything — just get comfortable with what each symbol means and why we care.
Learning objectives.By the end you should be able to articulate the kinematics→dynamics transition, classify forward vs. inverse problems, and recognise each term in the fundamental equation.
Prerequisites.Kinematics (transformations, Jacobians), matrix calculus, Newton's laws, and basic numerical computation in MATLAB or Python.

1 From kinematics to dynamics: the essential transition

Kinematics gave you the geometric backbone — positions, velocities, accelerations, where the end-effector lives in space. Useful, but it only ever answers what and how relative to what. It is silent on the questions that matter the moment you build hardware: how much torque does this motor need? Why does the arm overshoot at high speed? What happens when I bolt a 5 kg payload to the wrist?

Dynamics is the layer that answers what forces and torques cause this motion. The analogy: kinematics is the route on a map; dynamics is the fuel, the engine power, and the gradient of the hill. Both matter, but only the second one decides whether the journey is physically possible.

It is also a strict superset. Every dynamic analysis still uses positions, velocities, and accelerations — it just adds mass, inertia, friction, and the nonlinear coupling they introduce.

If you remember nothing else: kinematics is geometry, dynamics is physics. The robot doesn't care about your blueprints — it cares about Newton's second law.
Key insight.A change in one joint's motion produces forces at all the other joints. That coupling is the single biggest reason robot dynamics is harder than single-body mechanics.
Kinematics vs. dynamics at a glance
Aspect              Kinematics                         Dynamics
-----------------   --------------------------------   ----------------------------------------

2 Why dynamic modelling is non-negotiable

Dynamic modelling is not academic decoration — it is the difference between a robot that works on a benchtop and one that works on a factory floor. Without a dynamic model you cannot design controllers, you cannot pick actuators, and you cannot optimise energy or cycle time.

The physics underneath is just $\mathbf{F} = m\mathbf{a}$, but in a multi-link manipulator that simple law explodes into a system of coupled, nonlinear ODEs. Four effects are responsible:

  • Inertial coupling — accelerating joint 1 means accelerating every link downstream of it, and the effective inertia changes radically with posture.
  • Gravitational loading — a horizontal link experiences maximum gravitational torque, a vertical one almost none, and this shifts continuously as the robot moves.
  • Velocity-dependent forces — Coriolis and centrifugal terms appear only when joints move together, and at high speed they can demand 20–30% of total actuator capacity.
  • Configuration dependency — the inertia seen by an actuator can change by an order of magnitude between an extended and a folded posture.

The punchline: intuitive shortcuts — "just turn up the gains" or "add more torque" — fail spectacularly when speed and precision both rise.

Moving a pencil and moving a 100 kg car panel might trace the same path. The dynamics are nothing alike.
Newton's second law, weaponised.$\mathbf{F}=m\mathbf{a}$ in a serial manipulator is not one equation — it is $n$ coupled, nonlinear, configuration-dependent equations. That coupling is the reason this course exists.
Where it bites in practice.Precision manufacturing (vibration, finish, payload variation) and surgical robotics (force feedback, tremor-free motion, safety) both fail outright without dynamic compensation.

3 Case study: a 6-DOF automotive welding robot

A 6-DOF arm welds car body panels at speeds up to 1.5 m/s with a positional tolerance of ±0.1 mm. Kinematics alone gets nowhere near that spec. Three dynamic effects have to be compensated:

1. Inertial overshoot. Rapid acceleration develops massive inertial forces; pure position feedback can't react fast enough. Feedforward inverse dynamics computes the torques needed before the error appears, using the inertia matrix $\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})$ multiplied by the desired $\ddot{\mathbf{q}}$.

2. Gravitational sag. An extended horizontal arm droops by millimetres under its own weight — far outside the ±0.1 mm budget. Real-time computation of $\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q})$ cancels gravity continuously, making the structure appear weightless to the position loop.

3. Velocity-dependent path deviation. Coriolis and centrifugal forces $\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}})$ pull the tool off curved paths at speed. Velocity feedforward compensates them in real time.

Quantitatively, fully-modelled dynamic compensation took accuracy from ±0.5 mm to ±0.05 mm (10×), cut cycle time by 40%, dropped energy use by 25%, and reduced scrap rates by 85%. The model paid for itself inside six months.

This is the example to keep in your head all term. Every term we derive corresponds directly to one of these three failures.
What each term buys you.$\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})\ddot{\mathbf{q}}$ kills overshoot. $\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q})$ cancels sag. $\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}})$ keeps you on the curve at speed.

4 Forward vs. inverse dynamics

Both problems use the same physics — they just walk through it in opposite directions.

  • Forward dynamics — given applied torques $\boldsymbol{\tau}(t)$, find the resulting accelerations $\ddot{\mathbf{q}}(t)$. Used for simulation, prediction, verification, safety monitoring. Computationally heavier (it requires inverting $\mathbf{D}$).
  • Inverse dynamics — given a desired trajectory $\mathbf{q}(t),\dot{\mathbf{q}}(t),\ddot{\mathbf{q}}(t)$, find the torques $\boldsymbol{\tau}(t)$ that produce it. Used for real-time control, actuator sizing, feedforward compensation. Direct evaluation — no inversion needed.

Think of it as prediction vs. planning. Forward asks "if I push like this, what happens?" Inverse asks "to make this happen, how should I push?" In a real robot they run side by side: inverse dynamics drives the feedforward path, forward dynamics drives the simulator and the safety monitor.

Memorise the direction of each arrow. Mixing them up is the single most common confusion in this whole course.
Why both, always.You verify a controller in simulation (forward dynamics) before you deploy it; you execute the controller on hardware using inverse dynamics. The same model serves both.
Forward vs. inverse — same equation, two directions
Forward dynamics    (given τ → find q̈):
    q̈  =  D⁻¹(q) · [ τ  −  h(q, q̇)  −  c(q) ]

Inverse dynamics    (given q̈ → find τ):
    τ  =  D(q)·q̈  +  h(q, q̇)  +  c(q)
Surgical-robot dual usage
Pre-op planning   →  inverse dynamics  →  τ_feedforward
Intra-op monitor  →  forward dynamics  →  q̈_predicted vs. q̈_measured

5 The fundamental equation and its three components

Every result in this course rests on one equation:

$$\boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})\ddot{\mathbf{q}} + \mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}}) + \mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q})$$

Three terms, three physical effects, three things actuators have to fight against.

Inertia matrix $\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})$ — $n\times n$, symmetric, positive definite. The effective inertia each actuator sees; off-diagonal entries encode coupling between joints. It changes with posture — that single fact is responsible for most of the difficulty in robot control.

Coriolis & centrifugal vector $\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}})$ — $n\times 1$, quadratic in velocity ($\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\alpha\dot{\mathbf{q}}) = \alpha^2\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}})$). Coriolis from joint–joint interaction, centrifugal from a joint's own motion. Negligible at low speed, dominant at high speed.

Gravity vector $\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q})$ — $n\times 1$, position-dependent, velocity-independent. A conservative force derivable from the system's potential energy — which is exactly the door we'll walk through when we introduce Lagrangian mechanics.

Stare at this equation. By the end of the course you'll be able to derive every symbol on the right-hand side from first principles — but right now, just learn to read it.
Inertia D(q).Symmetric, positive definite, configuration-dependent. Off-diagonals = inter-joint coupling.
Coriolis/centrifugal h(q,q̇).Quadratic in velocity. Zero at rest, savage at speed.
Gravity c(q).Pure function of pose. The only term you can compensate without ever measuring velocity.

      inertial          velocity-dependent          gravity

6 Mathematical properties that control engineers exploit

The equation has structure, and that structure is gold for control design.

Symmetry & positive definiteness of $\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})$ — $\mathbf{D}=\mathbf{D}^T$ and $\mathbf{x}^T\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})\mathbf{x}>0$ for all $\mathbf{x}\neq 0$. This guarantees energy conservation and underwrites stability proofs.

Skew-symmetry property — $\dot{\mathbf{D}}(\mathbf{q}) - 2\mathbf{C}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}})$ is skew-symmetric. This passivity identity is the single most useful fact in adaptive and energy-based robot control — Lyapunov proofs collapse around it.

Boundedness — $\mathbf{D}$ is bounded above and below for all configurations; $\mathbf{h}$ is bounded by quadratic functions of velocity; $\mathbf{c}$ is bounded for all finite poses. These bounds let you certify worst-case actuator demands and design robust controllers.

We will lean on every one of these properties when we get to control design — they are the reason robot control is tractable despite the nonlinearity.

These aren't trivia — they're the reason a Lyapunov argument exists at all for a system this nonlinear.
Why passivity matters.The skew-symmetry of $\dot{\mathbf{D}} - 2\mathbf{C}$ means the manipulator is energy-conserving in a precise sense, which is what lets adaptive controllers prove stability without knowing the exact parameters.
The three properties in one place

7 How dynamics plugs into a real control loop

A modern manipulator controller is a partnership: feedforward (driven by inverse dynamics) does the heavy lifting, feedback handles whatever is left over — disturbances, modelling error, payload uncertainty.

The canonical architecture:

  1. Trajectory planner generates $\mathbf{q}_d(t),\dot{\mathbf{q}}_d(t),\ddot{\mathbf{q}}_d(t)$.
  2. Inverse dynamics (feedforward) computes $\boldsymbol{\tau}_{ff} = \mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})\ddot{\mathbf{q}}_d + \mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}}) + \mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q})$.
  3. Feedback adds $\boldsymbol{\tau}_{fb}$ to correct tracking error.
  4. Total command $\boldsymbol{\tau}_{total} = \boldsymbol{\tau}_{ff} + \boldsymbol{\tau}_{fb}$ goes to the actuators.
  5. Plant (forward dynamics) produces the actual motion.

The better the model in step 2, the smaller the error feedback has to clean up — which directly translates into higher gains being safe, faster motions being stable, and tighter tracking being achievable. Every kilogram of accuracy in $\mathbf{D}$, $\mathbf{h}$, and $\mathbf{c}$ shows up at the tool tip.

Bad models force feedback to do all the work. Good models let feedback specialise in the things models can't predict.
Rule of thumb.If your robot is sluggish or oscillates, suspect the model, not the gains. Feedback can mask a poor model only up to a point — and that point is where high-performance robotics actually starts.
