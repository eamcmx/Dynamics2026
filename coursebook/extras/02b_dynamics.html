Lecture 02 · Module 2 · Robot Manipulator Dynamics

Energy-Based Approach to Robot Dynamics

Lagrangian mechanics and the systematic path from kinetic & potential energy to the equations of motion of a manipulator.

Lecturer. bibliosense Lecture. 02 Course. Robot Manipulator Dynamics
In this module we step away from free-body diagrams and constraint bookkeeping, and let energy do the heavy lifting. We'll see why scalar quantities — kinetic and potential energy — give us a far cleaner route to the dynamics of multi-link manipulators than tracking every interaction force. By the end you should be able to write the Lagrangian of a robot, turn the crank on the Euler–Lagrange equations, and recognise the inertia, Coriolis and gravity terms in the standard manipulator equation.

0What this lecture covers

This lecture builds the energy-based view of manipulator dynamics. We start by contrasting force methods (Newton–Euler) with energy methods (Lagrangian) and motivate why energy wins as soon as the kinematic chain gets non-trivial. We then develop the two ingredients of the Lagrangian — kinetic energy $T$ and potential energy $V$ — for multi-link robots, introduce generalized coordinates and the Euler–Lagrange equations, and finish by showing how the whole machinery collapses naturally into the standard manipulator equation $\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})\ddot{\mathbf{q}} + \mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}}) + \mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q}) = \boldsymbol{\tau}$.

By the end of this lecture you should see the manipulator equation not as a formula to memorise, but as something that falls out of $L = T - V$ almost on its own.
Learning objectives. Understand energy in multi-link systems, master the Lagrangian framework, see why energy beats force methods on complex robots, work in generalized coordinates, and apply the Euler–Lagrange equations to derive manipulator dynamics.

1Why energy methods beat force methods

Newton–Euler and Lagrangian formulations are mathematically equivalent — they give you the same equations of motion. The difference is the path you take to get there. For a single pendulum or a free particle, force methods are intuitive: draw the free-body diagram, sum forces, apply $\mathbf{F} = m\mathbf{a}$. But on a multi-link arm the force approach drowns you in vectors, coordinate frames, and constraint forces at every joint that don't even contribute to motion.

Energy methods sidestep all of this. Kinetic and potential energy are scalars, so we don't have to juggle vector frames. Constraint forces from ideal joints disappear automatically from the final equations. And the procedure is mechanical: write $T$, write $V$, form $L = T - V$, turn the Euler–Lagrange crank.

Force methods make you a bookkeeper of every push and pull in the chain. Energy methods let you ignore everything that cancels out.
Force-based (Newton–Euler). Free-body diagram per link, force/moment balance per link, explicit constraint forces at joints, coupled vector equations — easy to make sign or coupling errors on a long chain.
Energy-based (Lagrangian). Scalar energies, automatic constraint elimination, generalized coordinates of your choice, and a systematic procedure that's easy to verify and even automate symbolically.
3-DOF arm — concrete contrast. Force route: 6 balance equations across 3 free bodies plus joint constraints. Energy route: 3 kinetic + 3 potential scalar expressions, one Lagrangian, one mechanical procedure. Same answer, far less pain.

2Kinetic and potential energy in multi-link systems

Robot kinetic energy is not just $\tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$. Every link generally has both translational kinetic energy (from its centre-of-mass velocity) and rotational kinetic energy (from its spin about its centre of mass). Worse, the velocities are coupled through the chain: when joint 1 moves, every downstream link moves with it.

For a robot with $n$ links the total kinetic energy is

$$T = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \left[ m_i\,\mathbf{v}_i^{T}\mathbf{v}_i + \boldsymbol{\omega}_i^{T}\,\mathbf{I}_i\,\boldsymbol{\omega}_i \right],$$

where $\mathbf{v}_i$ and $\boldsymbol{\omega}_i$ depend on all joint velocities up to link $i$.

Potential energy is gentler — it's purely configuration-dependent. In a uniform gravitational field,

$$V = \sum_{i=1}^{n} m_i\,\mathbf{g}^{T}\mathbf{r}_i,$$

with $\mathbf{g} \approx [0,\,0,\,-9.81]^{T}$ m/s² and $\mathbf{r}_i$ the position of link $i$'s centre of mass. An extended horizontal arm sits at high $V$; the same arm folded vertically sits much lower — and the difference is exactly the gravitational torque the actuators have to fight.

Two scalars per link, summed over the chain. That's the entire energetic description of the robot — no vectors floating around yet.
Velocity coupling. $\mathbf{v}_i$ and $\boldsymbol{\omega}_i$ both depend on the velocities of all joints between the base and link $i$. That coupling is exactly what will produce the off-diagonal inertia terms later.
Reference matters. Choose your reference height for $V$ to make the algebra clean. The dynamics only care about differences in potential energy, so a clever zero saves a lot of ink.

3The Lagrangian and generalized coordinates

Lagrangian mechanics gives us a systematic recipe for turning energies into equations of motion. The key trick is the use of generalized coordinates — any independent set of parameters that fully specifies the configuration of the system. For robots, the natural choice is the vector of joint variables $\mathbf{q}$ (angles for revolute joints, displacements for prismatic ones). A robot with $n$ joints has $n$ degrees of freedom and needs exactly $n$ generalized coordinates.

The Lagrangian is then defined as

$$L(\mathbf{q},\,\dot{\mathbf{q}},\,t) \;=\; T(\mathbf{q},\,\dot{\mathbf{q}}) \;-\; V(\mathbf{q}).$$

Physically, $L$ is the excess of kinetic over potential energy. The principle of least action says the actual motion of the system extremizes the time integral of $L$ — and the equations that fall out of that variational principle are the Euler–Lagrange equations.

Joint variables are the right coordinates because they're what the actuators actually drive. The maths and the engineering line up.
Why generalized coordinates. Minimal representation, automatic satisfaction of joint constraints, and a direct correspondence to what the motors are commanding. Three wins for the price of a definition.

4The Euler–Lagrange equations

Once you have $L = T - V$, the equations of motion come from the Euler–Lagrange equations, one per generalized coordinate:

$$\frac{d}{dt}\!\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot{q}_i}\right) - \frac{\partial L}{\partial q_i} \;=\; \tau_i, \qquad i = 1,\ldots,n,$$

where $\tau_i$ is the generalized force conjugate to $q_i$ — for a revolute joint, the joint torque; for a prismatic joint, the joint force.

The procedure is the same every time:

  1. Express $T$ and $V$ in terms of $\mathbf{q}$ and $\dot{\mathbf{q}}$.
  2. Form the Lagrangian $L = T - V$.
  3. Compute $\partial L / \partial \dot q_i$, take its time derivative, then subtract $\partial L / \partial q_i$.
  4. Set the result equal to $\tau_i$.
  5. Tidy up.

That's it. The same recipe works for a 1-DOF pendulum and for a 7-DOF industrial arm.

Memorise the recipe, not the answer. The recipe is what generalises.
Worked example: 2-DOF planar arm. Take $q_1 = \theta_1$, $q_2 = \theta_2$. Each link contributes translational and rotational $T$; link 2's velocity couples to $\dot\theta_1$. Heights of the centres of mass give $V$. Plug $L = T - V$ into Euler–Lagrange and you get coupled, nonlinear ODEs containing inertial coupling, Coriolis/centrifugal terms, and gravity loading — all from the same crank.
Even simple is messy. A 2-DOF planar arm already produces nontrivially coupled nonlinear equations. That complexity isn't a flaw of the Lagrangian — it's the actual physics of multi-body systems.

5Velocity kinematics for kinetic energy

To actually evaluate $T$, we need the linear and angular velocities of every link, expressed in terms of the joint velocities $\dot{\mathbf{q}}$.

Linear velocity. If $\mathbf{r}_i(\mathbf{q})$ is the position of link $i$'s centre of mass, then the chain rule gives

$$\mathbf{v}_i \;=\; \sum_{j=1}^{i} \frac{\partial \mathbf{r}_i}{\partial q_j}\,\dot q_j \;=\; \mathbf{J}_{vi}(\mathbf{q})\,\dot{\mathbf{q}},$$

where $\mathbf{J}_{vi}$ is the linear-velocity Jacobian of link $i$'s centre of mass.

Angular velocity. Each revolute joint $j$ contributes $\dot q_j$ to the angular velocity of every link beyond it; prismatic joints contribute nothing rotationally. So angular velocities just add up along the chain:

  • $\boldsymbol{\omega}_1 = \dot q_1$
  • $\boldsymbol{\omega}_2 = \dot q_1 + \dot q_2$
  • $\boldsymbol{\omega}_3 = \dot q_1 + \dot q_2 + \dot q_3$

(for a 3-DOF planar revolute arm).

Once every $\mathbf{v}_i$ and $\boldsymbol{\omega}_i$ is linear in $\dot{\mathbf{q}}$, the kinetic energy becomes a quadratic form in $\dot{\mathbf{q}}$ — and that's where the inertia matrix is born.

Velocities aren't independent — they cascade down the chain. Get this step right and the rest is bookkeeping.
Inertia matrix. $T = \tfrac{1}{2}\,\dot{\mathbf{q}}^{T}\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})\,\dot{\mathbf{q}}$, with $D_{ij}(\mathbf{q}) = \sum_{k=1}^{n} \!\left[ m_k\!\left(\tfrac{\partial \mathbf{r}_k}{\partial q_i}\right)^{T}\!\!\left(\tfrac{\partial \mathbf{r}_k}{\partial q_j}\right) + \!\left(\tfrac{\partial \boldsymbol{\omega}_k}{\partial q_i}\right)^{T}\!\mathbf{I}_k\!\left(\tfrac{\partial \boldsymbol{\omega}_k}{\partial q_j}\right)\right]$. Diagonal terms are the effective inertia at each joint; off-diagonals are the inertial coupling between joints.
Properties of $\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})$. Always symmetric and positive definite. Configuration-dependent — that's why robot dynamics are nonlinear even with linear actuators.

6From Lagrangian to the manipulator equation

Now we apply Euler–Lagrange to $L = T - V$ in the matrix form. Differentiating $T = \tfrac{1}{2}\dot{\mathbf{q}}^{T}\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})\dot{\mathbf{q}}$ and $V(\mathbf{q})$ and pushing through the algebra produces, after collecting terms by physical meaning, the standard manipulator equation:

$$\boxed{\;\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})\,\ddot{\mathbf{q}} + \mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}}) + \mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q}) \;=\; \boldsymbol{\tau}\;}$$

Three terms, three physical interpretations:

  • $\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})\ddot{\mathbf{q}}$ — inertial term, comes straight from the kinetic energy.
  • $\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}})$ — Coriolis and centrifugal term, born from the time derivatives of $\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})$.
  • $\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q})$ — gravity term, $c_i = \partial V/\partial q_i$.

The Coriolis components have the explicit Christoffel-symbol form

$$h_i = \sum_{j,k} h_{ijk}\,\dot q_j \dot q_k,\qquad h_{ijk} = \tfrac{1}{2}\!\left(\tfrac{\partial D_{ij}}{\partial q_k} + \tfrac{\partial D_{ik}}{\partial q_j} - \tfrac{\partial D_{jk}}{\partial q_i}\right).$$

Every industrial manipulator controller you'll ever meet is, at its core, computing exactly these three terms.

Inertia, Coriolis, gravity — three buckets, one equation, every robot.
$\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})$ — inertia. From $T$. Symmetric, positive definite, configuration-dependent. Encodes both effective joint inertias and inertial coupling.
$\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}})$ — Coriolis & centrifugal. Velocity-quadratic terms from $\dot{\mathbf{D}}(\mathbf{q})$. They're what couple joint motions into each other dynamically — felt strongly at high speed.
$\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q})$ — gravity. Comes directly from $V$ via $c_i = \partial V/\partial q_i$. Configuration-dependent torques the actuators must hold against, even at rest.
Manipulator equation in standard form
D(q) q̈  +  h(q, q̇)  +  c(q)  =  τ

  D(q)        : inertia matrix          (from T)
  h(q, q̇)    : Coriolis / centrifugal  (from dD/dt)
  c(q)        : gravity vector          (from V)
  τ           : joint torque/force vector

7Why this approach wins for robotics

Stepping back, the Lagrangian route gives us several concrete wins over force-based derivations:

  • Systematic procedure. The same five steps work for any robot, any DOF count, any mix of revolute and prismatic joints.
  • Physical insight. Each term in $\mathbf{D}\ddot{\mathbf{q}} + \mathbf{h} + \mathbf{c} = \boldsymbol{\tau}$ has a clear meaning, so we can reason about the physics, not just the algebra.
  • Computational structure. The quadratic-form structure of $T$ and the symmetry of $\mathbf{D}$ enable efficient $O(n)$ recursive algorithms in real-time controllers.
  • Error reduction. Far fewer sign and coupling mistakes than free-body bookkeeping on a long chain.
  • Automation. The whole derivation is symbolic and mechanical — symbolic-math packages can do it for you.

In industry, real-time controllers compute $\mathbf{D}$, $\mathbf{h}$, and $\mathbf{c}$ at 1 kHz or more. Switching from naive PID to model-based control built on this framework typically buys 20–40% energy savings, sub-millimetre accuracy at speed, and 2–3× faster cycle times.

The systematic structure isn't an academic luxury — it's why model-based controllers beat tuned PID in factories worldwide.
Industrial reality. A typical automotive welding cell going from PID to a Lagrangian-based dynamic-model controller: ±2 mm → ±0.2 mm accuracy, 45 s → 28 s cycle time, 25% energy savings. Same hardware, better dynamics.

8Self-assessment: 2-DOF planar arm

A 2-link planar arm with both link masses concentrated at the distal end of each link. Parameters: link 1 of length $L_1$ with mass $m_1$ at its tip; link 2 of length $L_2$ with mass $m_2$ at its tip; joint angles $\theta_1, \theta_2$. Neglect link mass distribution and link inertia (all mass at tips).

Work through the following — it's the canonical first Lagrangian derivation in robotics, and you should be able to do it on autopilot by the end of the module.

  1. Kinematics. Write the position of each tip mass as a function of $L_1, L_2, \theta_1, \theta_2$.
  2. Velocities. Differentiate using the chain rule to get $\dot{\mathbf{r}}_1$ and $\dot{\mathbf{r}}_2$ in terms of $\dot\theta_1, \dot\theta_2$.
  3. Kinetic energy. Form $T = \tfrac{1}{2} m_1 \dot{\mathbf{r}}_1^{T}\dot{\mathbf{r}}_1 + \tfrac{1}{2} m_2 \dot{\mathbf{r}}_2^{T}\dot{\mathbf{r}}_2$.
  4. Potential energy. With gravity in the $-y$ direction, write $V$ in terms of the heights of $m_1$ and $m_2$.
  5. Lagrangian. Form $L = T - V$. (You don't have to crank Euler–Lagrange yet — just get $L$ clean.)
Draw your own schematic and label every angle, position, and velocity. The bookkeeping is the lesson.
Tip. If your $T$ ends up as a clean quadratic in $\dot\theta_1, \dot\theta_2$ with a $\cos(\theta_2)$ coupling term, you're on the right track — that cosine is the inertial coupling between the joints.

9Summary and what's next

You should now be comfortable with the energy-based view of manipulator dynamics:

  • Energy framework. $T = \tfrac{1}{2}\dot{\mathbf{q}}^{T}\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})\dot{\mathbf{q}}$, $V = \sum_i m_i\mathbf{g}^{T}\mathbf{r}_i$, $L = T - V$.
  • Generalized coordinates. Joint variables are the natural choice; they make constraints vanish.
  • Euler–Lagrange equations. A mechanical recipe that works for any DOF count.
  • Manipulator equation. $\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})\ddot{\mathbf{q}} + \mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}}) + \mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q}) = \boldsymbol{\tau}$ — inertia, Coriolis, gravity.

Coming up in Module 3 we'll do the full Lagrangian derivation end-to-end: building the link velocities systematically, computing $\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})$ element by element, extracting the Christoffel symbols for $\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}})$, handling $\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q})$ from $V$, and applying the whole machine to concrete manipulators.

Before you move on, make sure you're comfortable with matrix calculus (partial derivatives of matrix expressions), the link kinematics from your transformation matrices, and basic vector operations.

If you can explain to a colleague why each term in $\mathbf{D}\ddot{\mathbf{q}} + \mathbf{h} + \mathbf{c} = \boldsymbol{\tau}$ exists and where it comes from, you're ready for Module 3.
