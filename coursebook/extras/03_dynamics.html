Lecture 07 · Module 2 · Robotics: Dynamics & Control

Worked example — kinematics & energies of a 2-link planar arm

From transformation matrices to the Lagrangian, one careful step at a time.

Lecturer. bibliosense Lecture. 07
This one is a long, deliberate worked example — no new theory, just the bookkeeping. We take a 2-link planar arm with a point mass at the tip of each link and walk it all the way from the homogeneous transforms down to a clean Lagrangian. The point of the exercise is the grouping: when you square the velocities and use $\sin^2+\cos^2=1$ and $\cos(\alpha-\beta)$ identities, the mess collapses into something you can actually feed into Euler-Lagrange next module.

0 What this lecture covers

We work a single problem from start to finish: a 2-link planar robot arm with tip masses $m_1$ and $m_2$ at the distal end of each link, link lengths $L_1, L_2$, joint angles $\theta_1, \theta_2$. The goal is to derive the Lagrangian $L = T - V$ that we will hand to Euler-Lagrange in Module 3.

The path is fixed and worth memorising:

  1. Write each link's homogeneous transformation.
  2. Read off Cartesian positions of the tip masses.
  3. Differentiate to get velocities.
  4. Square and simplify to get $v^2$.
  5. Assemble kinetic energy $T$, potential energy $V$, and finally $L = T-V$.
No new physics today — just the careful algebra that earns you the right to use Euler-Lagrange next time.
Setup. Planar arm in the $xy$-plane, gravity along $-y$. Tip masses are point masses (no link inertia) — that simplification is what keeps the algebra tractable.

1 Homogeneous transformations for each link

We start with the base-to-link transforms. In 2D a homogeneous transform is a $3\times 3$ matrix: rotation in the upper-left $2\times 2$ block, translation in the last column.

For link 1 (rotation by $\theta_1$, translation along the link of length $L_1$):

$$T_1 = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta_1 & -\sin\theta_1 & L_1\cos\theta_1 \\ \sin\theta_1 & \cos\theta_1 & L_1\sin\theta_1 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

For link 2, expressed relative to the base (already composed through link 1), the rotation accumulates to $\theta_1+\theta_2$ and the translation is the sum of both link contributions:

$$T_2 = \begin{bmatrix} \cos(\theta_1+\theta_2) & -\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2) & L_1\cos\theta_1 + L_2\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2) \\ \sin(\theta_1+\theta_2) & \cos(\theta_1+\theta_2) & L_1\sin\theta_1 + L_2\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2) \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

$T_2$ is what you get from $T_1 \cdot T_{1\to 2}$ — we just write the result so we don't carry the multiplication clutter through the rest of the derivation.
Why homogeneous? Stacking rotation and translation into one matrix lets us compose link frames by multiplication. The bottom row $[0,0,1]$ is the bookkeeping that makes that work.

2 Cartesian positions of the tip masses

The Cartesian position of each tip mass is just the translation column of its transform.

Mass $m_1$ (end of link 1):

$$x_1 = L_1\cos\theta_1, \qquad y_1 = L_1\sin\theta_1$$

Mass $m_2$ (end of link 2):

$$x_2 = L_1\cos\theta_1 + L_2\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2)$$

$$y_2 = L_1\sin\theta_1 + L_2\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2)$$

This is forward kinematics in two equations. Notice that $m_2$'s position depends on both joint angles — that coupling is what will make the velocities (and ultimately the dynamics) interesting.

3 Velocities by the chain rule

Differentiate each position with respect to time. The angles are the time-varying coordinates, so we use the chain rule and pick up $\dot\theta_1$ and $\dot\theta_2$ factors.

For $m_1$:

$$\dot{x}_1 = -L_1\sin\theta_1\,\dot{\theta}_1, \qquad \dot{y}_1 = L_1\cos\theta_1\,\dot{\theta}_1$$

For $m_2$:

$$\dot{x}_2 = -L_1\sin\theta_1\,\dot{\theta}_1 - L_2\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2)\,(\dot{\theta}_1+\dot{\theta}_2)$$

$$\dot{y}_2 = L_1\cos\theta_1\,\dot{\theta}_1 + L_2\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2)\,(\dot{\theta}_1+\dot{\theta}_2)$$

The key chain-rule moment is differentiating $\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2)$ — its time derivative is $-\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2)\cdot(\dot\theta_1+\dot\theta_2)$, not $-\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2)\cdot\dot\theta_2$. Forgetting the $\dot\theta_1$ inside that bracket is the single most common mistake on this problem.

If you take nothing else away from this lecture, take the bracket $(\dot\theta_1 + \dot\theta_2)$ on the link-2 velocity terms. That sum is the angular velocity of link 2 in the world frame, and you cannot drop the $\dot\theta_1$.
Common slip. Many students write $-L_2\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2)\,\dot\theta_2$ instead of $-L_2\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2)\,(\dot\theta_1+\dot\theta_2)$. The chain rule applies to the whole argument of the trig function.

4 Squared speed of $m_1$

Square the velocity components and add. For $m_1$ this is short and clean:

$$v_1^2 = \dot{x}_1^2 + \dot{y}_1^2 = L_1^2\dot\theta_1^2\sin^2\theta_1 + L_1^2\dot\theta_1^2\cos^2\theta_1$$

Factor out $L_1^2\dot\theta_1^2$ and use the Pythagorean identity $\sin^2\theta_1 + \cos^2\theta_1 = 1$:

$$\boxed{\,v_1^2 = L_1^2\dot\theta_1^2\,}$$

That is exactly what intuition expects: the tip of a rigid link of length $L_1$ rotating at angular speed $\dot\theta_1$ moves at linear speed $L_1\dot\theta_1$.

Sanity check. $v = L\dot\theta$ for a point on a rotating arm — the algebra recovers the textbook answer, which is the cheapest way to confirm we set up the transforms correctly.

5 Squared speed of $m_2$ — the messy one

For $m_2$ we need to square both brackets and collect terms. Start by squaring each component:

$$v_2^2 = (\dot{x}_2)^2 + (\dot{y}_2)^2$$

Expanding $(\dot x_2)^2$ and $(\dot y_2)^2$ produces six terms each, but after pairing the $x$- and $y$-contributions:

$$\begin{aligned} v_2^2 =\;& L_1^2\sin^2\theta_1\,\dot\theta_1^2 + L_2^2\sin^2(\theta_1+\theta_2)(\dot\theta_1+\dot\theta_2)^2 \\ & + 2L_1L_2\sin\theta_1\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2)\,\dot\theta_1(\dot\theta_1+\dot\theta_2) \\ & + L_1^2\cos^2\theta_1\,\dot\theta_1^2 + L_2^2\cos^2(\theta_1+\theta_2)(\dot\theta_1+\dot\theta_2)^2 \\ & + 2L_1L_2\cos\theta_1\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2)\,\dot\theta_1(\dot\theta_1+\dot\theta_2) \end{aligned}$$

Now apply two identities:

  • Pythagoras: $\sin^2\alpha + \cos^2\alpha = 1$ — collapses the $L_1^2$ and $L_2^2$ pairs.
  • Cosine of difference: $\sin\alpha\sin\beta + \cos\alpha\cos\beta = \cos(\alpha - \beta)$. With $\alpha=\theta_1$ and $\beta=\theta_1+\theta_2$, the difference is $-\theta_2$ and $\cos(-\theta_2) = \cos\theta_2$.

The whole thing collapses to:

$$\boxed{\,v_2^2 = L_1^2\dot\theta_1^2 + L_2^2(\dot\theta_1+\dot\theta_2)^2 + 2L_1L_2\cos\theta_2\,\dot\theta_1(\dot\theta_1+\dot\theta_2)\,}$$

This is the moment the algebra pays off — six pages of expansion fold down to three terms, and the cross-term carries a $\cos\theta_2$ that will become the coupling between the two joints in the dynamics.
The coupling term. The last term, $2L_1L_2\cos\theta_2\,\dot\theta_1(\dot\theta_1+\dot\theta_2)$, is what makes a 2-link arm a coupled dynamical system. When $\theta_2 = \pm 90°$, the coupling vanishes and the joints decouple instantaneously.
Sign of the angle. $\cos(-\theta_2) = \cos\theta_2$ because cosine is even. If we had ended up with sines, we would have picked up a minus sign — worth slowing down on.

6 Total kinetic energy $T$

With the squared speeds in hand, the kinetic energy is just $T = \tfrac{1}{2}m_1 v_1^2 + \tfrac{1}{2}m_2 v_2^2$:

$$T = \tfrac{1}{2} m_1 L_1^2\dot\theta_1^2 + \tfrac{1}{2} m_2 \left[\,L_1^2\dot\theta_1^2 + L_2^2(\dot\theta_1+\dot\theta_2)^2 + 2L_1L_2\cos\theta_2\,\dot\theta_1(\dot\theta_1+\dot\theta_2)\,\right]$$

Note the structure: $T$ is quadratic in the joint velocities $\dot\theta_1, \dot\theta_2$ and depends on the configuration only through $\cos\theta_2$. That is the generic form $T = \tfrac12 \dot q^\top M(q) \dot q$ you will see throughout the rest of the course — $M(q)$ is the mass matrix, and you can read its entries straight off this expression.

Looking ahead. The mass matrix here is $\begin{bmatrix} m_1L_1^2 + m_2(L_1^2 + L_2^2 + 2L_1L_2\cos\theta_2) & m_2(L_2^2 + L_1L_2\cos\theta_2) \\ m_2(L_2^2 + L_1L_2\cos\theta_2) & m_2 L_2^2 \end{bmatrix}$ — keep an eye out for it next module.

7 Potential energy $V$

Take $y$ as the upward direction and gravity $g$ pointing down. Each tip mass contributes $mgy$:

$$V = m_1 g y_1 + m_2 g y_2$$

Substituting the positions:

$$V = m_1 g L_1\sin\theta_1 + m_2 g \left[L_1\sin\theta_1 + L_2\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2)\right]$$

Unlike $T$, $V$ depends only on the configuration (the angles), not on the velocities. That is exactly what we expect from a conservative gravitational potential.

You can group the $L_1\sin\theta_1$ terms if you like — $(m_1+m_2)gL_1\sin\theta_1 + m_2 g L_2\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2)$ is the same equation, sometimes easier to differentiate.
Reference height. We measure $y$ from the base joint. The choice is arbitrary — adding a constant to $V$ does not change the equations of motion.

8 Assemble the Lagrangian $L = T - V$

With $T$ and $V$ in hand, the Lagrangian is simply their difference:

$$L = T - V$$

Written out:

$$L = \tfrac{1}{2} m_1 L_1^2\dot\theta_1^2 + \tfrac{1}{2} m_2 \left[L_1^2\dot\theta_1^2 + L_2^2(\dot\theta_1+\dot\theta_2)^2 + 2L_1L_2\cos\theta_2\,\dot\theta_1(\dot\theta_1+\dot\theta_2)\right]$$

$$\;-\; m_1 g L_1\sin\theta_1 - m_2 g \left[L_1\sin\theta_1 + L_2\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2)\right]$$

This $L$ is the input to Euler-Lagrange in Module 3. From here, the equations of motion come from

$$\frac{d}{dt}\!\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot q_i}\right) - \frac{\partial L}{\partial q_i} = \tau_i$$

for $q_i \in \{\theta_1, \theta_2\}$. We will not crank that handle today — but you now have the object you need to do it.

Don't try to simplify $L$ any further. Its current form is the most useful form for taking the partials cleanly — collapse it now and you will regret it when you differentiate.
Stop here. It is tempting to plug straight into Euler-Lagrange. Resist — verify the $L$ you wrote against this lecture before proceeding, because every error in $L$ is multiplied by every partial derivative you take.

9 Recap & what to take into Module 3

You have walked the full pipeline:

  1. Transforms $T_1, T_2$ — encode geometry of the arm.
  2. Positions $(x_1,y_1), (x_2,y_2)$ — read off the translation column.
  3. Velocities — chain rule, with the $(\dot\theta_1+\dot\theta_2)$ bracket on link 2.
  4. Squared speeds — Pythagorean and cosine-of-difference identities collapse the mess.
  5. Kinetic energy $T$ — quadratic in velocities, encodes the mass matrix $M(q)$.
  6. Potential energy $V$ — only depends on configuration.
  7. Lagrangian $L = T - V$ — the input to Euler-Lagrange.

Next module we feed this $L$ into the Euler-Lagrange equations and read off the equations of motion — the mass matrix, Coriolis/centrifugal terms, and gravity vector all fall out of the partials we are about to take.
