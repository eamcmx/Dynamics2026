Worked Example: Kinematics & Energies of a 2-Link Planar Arm

From transformation matrices to the Lagrangian — the full derivation for a tip-mass planar robot.

Lecturer. bibliosense Lecture. 07
Today we walk a single problem all the way through. We have a 2-link planar arm with point masses at the tips, and we want the Lagrangian — because that's the gateway to the dynamics in Module 3. We'll start from the homogeneous transformation matrices, read off tip positions, differentiate to get velocities, square and simplify using the Pythagorean and angle-difference identities, then assemble $T$ and $V$. Take it slowly: every step here is a habit you'll re-use.

0 What this lecture covers

This lecture is a complete worked solution for a 2-link planar robot arm with tip masses $m_1$ and $m_2$ at the distal end of each link. We will build, step by step, every quantity needed to apply the Lagrangian formulation:

  • the homogeneous transformation matrices $T_1$ and $T_2$,
  • the Cartesian positions $(x_i, y_i)$ of each tip mass,
  • the velocities $(\dot{x}_i, \dot{y}_i)$ via the chain rule,
  • the squared speeds $v_i^2$ simplified using trig identities,
  • the kinetic energy $T$, the potential energy $V$, and finally the Lagrangian $L = T - V$.

By the end you will have an explicit, ready-to-differentiate $L(\theta_1, \theta_2, \dot{\theta}_1, \dot{\theta}_2)$ — the exact object Module 3 plugs into the Euler–Lagrange equations.

Treat this as a recipe card. Once you've done it once cleanly, every future manipulator becomes 'same steps, more terms'.
The setup. Two rigid links of length $L_1$ and $L_2$, joint angles $\theta_1$ (at the base) and $\theta_2$ (relative to link 1), with point masses $m_1$, $m_2$ at the tips.
Why this matters. Every dynamics problem in Module 3 starts exactly here: you cannot write Euler–Lagrange equations until you have $T$ and $V$ as functions of the generalised coordinates.

1 Transformation matrices for each link

We describe the geometry of the arm with homogeneous transformations. The transformation from the base frame to the tip of link 1 rotates by $\theta_1$ and translates by $L_1$ along the rotated axis:

$$T_1 = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta_1 & -\sin\theta_1 & L_1\cos\theta_1 \\ \sin\theta_1 & \cos\theta_1 & L_1\sin\theta_1 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

The transformation from the base frame to the tip of link 2 (where $m_2$ sits) composes the two rotations and adds both translations:

$$T_2 = \begin{bmatrix} \cos(\theta_1+\theta_2) & -\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2) & L_1\cos\theta_1 + L_2\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2) \\ \sin(\theta_1+\theta_2) & \cos(\theta_1+\theta_2) & L_1\sin\theta_1 + L_2\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2) \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

Notice that the rotation block of $T_2$ is by $\theta_1+\theta_2$ — angles add when frames are chained.

The third column is what you actually care about: it's the position of the tip in base coordinates.
Reading the matrix. For a 2D homogeneous transform, the top-left $2\times 2$ block is the rotation, and the third column $(\,p_x, p_y, 1\,)^T$ is the translation in base coordinates.

2 Tip positions $(x_i, y_i)$

Reading the third column of each transformation matrix directly gives the Cartesian positions of the masses.

For $m_1$ (end of link 1):

$$\begin{aligned} x_1 &= L_1\cos\theta_1 \\ y_1 &= L_1\sin\theta_1 \end{aligned}$$

For $m_2$ (end of link 2):

$$\begin{aligned} x_2 &= L_1\cos\theta_1 + L_2\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2) \\ y_2 &= L_1\sin\theta_1 + L_2\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2) \end{aligned}$$

These are pure functions of the generalised coordinates $\theta_1$ and $\theta_2$ — no time derivatives yet.

You can sanity-check by setting $\theta_2 = 0$: link 2 lines up with link 1 and $x_2 = (L_1+L_2)\cos\theta_1$. Good.

3 Velocities via the chain rule

Differentiate each position with respect to time. Because $x_i$ and $y_i$ depend on the joint angles, and the joint angles depend on time, the chain rule pulls $\dot\theta_1$ and $\dot\theta_2$ out of every trig term.

For $m_1$:

$$\begin{aligned} \dot{x}_1 &= -L_1\sin\theta_1\,\dot{\theta}_1 \\ \dot{y}_1 &= L_1\cos\theta_1\,\dot{\theta}_1 \end{aligned}$$

For $m_2$ — note that $(\theta_1+\theta_2)$ differentiates to $(\dot{\theta}_1+\dot{\theta}_2)$:

$$\begin{aligned} \dot{x}_2 &= -L_1\sin\theta_1\,\dot{\theta}_1 - L_2\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2)\,(\dot{\theta}_1+\dot{\theta}_2) \\ \dot{y}_2 &= L_1\cos\theta_1\,\dot{\theta}_1 + L_2\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2)\,(\dot{\theta}_1+\dot{\theta}_2) \end{aligned}$$

The second mass picks up two contributions: motion from joint 1 swinging the whole arm, and motion from joint 2 swinging link 2 about its base.

Don't drop the $(\dot\theta_1+\dot\theta_2)$ factor — that's the single most common mistake on this kind of derivation.
Watch the inner derivative. $\dfrac{d}{dt}\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2) = \cos(\theta_1+\theta_2)\,(\dot\theta_1+\dot\theta_2)$. Both angles are functions of time.

4 Squared speed of $m_1$

Square and add the velocity components. The $\sin^2 + \cos^2 = 1$ identity collapses the result instantly:

$$v_1^2 = \dot{x}_1^2 + \dot{y}_1^2 = (-L_1\sin\theta_1\,\dot{\theta}_1)^2 + (L_1\cos\theta_1\,\dot{\theta}_1)^2$$

$$= L_1^2\dot{\theta}_1^2(\sin^2\theta_1 + \cos^2\theta_1) = L_1^2\dot{\theta}_1^2$$

This is the classic result for a point mass at the end of a rotating rigid arm: speed is (arm length) × (angular rate).

If your derivation ever ends in something this clean, you're probably right.

5 Squared speed of $m_2$ — the algebra

This one is longer but uses exactly the same two identities. Start by squaring the velocity components:

$$v_2^2 = (\dot{x}_2)^2 + (\dot{y}_2)^2$$

Expanding both brackets gives six terms — three from each square:

$$\begin{aligned} v_2^2 = \;& L_1^2\sin^2\theta_1\,\dot{\theta}_1^2 + L_2^2\sin^2(\theta_1+\theta_2)\,(\dot{\theta}_1+\dot{\theta}_2)^2 \\ & + 2L_1L_2\sin\theta_1\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2)\,\dot{\theta}_1(\dot{\theta}_1+\dot{\theta}_2) \\ & + L_1^2\cos^2\theta_1\,\dot{\theta}_1^2 + L_2^2\cos^2(\theta_1+\theta_2)\,(\dot{\theta}_1+\dot{\theta}_2)^2 \\ & + 2L_1L_2\cos\theta_1\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2)\,\dot{\theta}_1(\dot{\theta}_1+\dot{\theta}_2) \end{aligned}$$

Now group with two identities:

  • $\sin^2\alpha + \cos^2\alpha = 1$ kills the pure-square pairs.
  • $\sin\alpha\sin\beta + \cos\alpha\cos\beta = \cos(\alpha - \beta)$ collapses the cross terms; here $\alpha-\beta = \theta_1 - (\theta_1+\theta_2) = -\theta_2$, and $\cos(-\theta_2) = \cos\theta_2$.

The whole expression collapses to:

$$\boxed{\,v_2^2 = L_1^2\dot{\theta}_1^2 + L_2^2(\dot{\theta}_1+\dot{\theta}_2)^2 + 2L_1L_2\cos\theta_2\,\dot{\theta}_1(\dot{\theta}_1+\dot{\theta}_2)\,}$$

The coupling between joints lives entirely inside that $\cos\theta_2$ term — when $\theta_2 = \pi/2$ the arm is folded square and the cross-coupling vanishes.

This collapse is the heart of the derivation. Memorise the two identities; everything else is bookkeeping.
The angle-difference identity. $\sin\alpha\sin\beta + \cos\alpha\cos\beta = \cos(\alpha-\beta)$ is the workhorse. Without it the cross terms stay ugly.
Physical meaning of $\cos\theta_2$. It measures how much the second link's motion projects onto the first link's swing direction. At $\theta_2 = 0$ the links are aligned and the coupling is maximal; at $\theta_2 = \pi/2$ they are perpendicular and the coupling is zero.

6 Total kinetic energy $T$

With both squared speeds in hand, the kinetic energy is just $\tfrac{1}{2}\sum m_i v_i^2$:

$$T = \tfrac{1}{2}m_1 v_1^2 + \tfrac{1}{2}m_2 v_2^2$$

Substituting the explicit expressions:

$$T = \tfrac{1}{2}m_1 L_1^2\dot{\theta}_1^2 + \tfrac{1}{2}m_2\!\left[\,L_1^2\dot{\theta}_1^2 + L_2^2(\dot{\theta}_1+\dot{\theta}_2)^2 + 2L_1L_2\cos\theta_2\,\dot{\theta}_1(\dot{\theta}_1+\dot{\theta}_2)\,\right]$$

Notice $T$ depends on $\theta_2$ (through $\cos\theta_2$) but not on $\theta_1$ — the kinetic energy is invariant under a rigid rotation of the whole arm about the base. That's a hint that $\theta_1$ may be a cyclic coordinate in the gravity-free case.

Whenever a coordinate doesn't appear in $T$ or $V$, the corresponding generalised momentum is conserved. Hold that thought for Module 3.
Mass matrix preview. Reading $T$ as $\tfrac{1}{2}\dot{\boldsymbol\theta}^T M(\boldsymbol\theta)\dot{\boldsymbol\theta}$, the $\cos\theta_2$ term becomes the off-diagonal entry of the manipulator's mass matrix $M$ — configuration-dependent inertia coupling.

7 Potential energy $V$

Take $y$ increasing upward and gravity $g$ acting downward. Each point mass contributes $m_i g y_i$:

$$V = m_1 g y_1 + m_2 g y_2$$

Substitute the position expressions from §2:

$$V = m_1 g L_1\sin\theta_1 + m_2 g\,[\,L_1\sin\theta_1 + L_2\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2)\,]$$

Unlike $T$, the potential energy depends on both angles — $\theta_1$ tells you how high mass 1 has been lifted, and the combination $\theta_1+\theta_2$ tells you how high mass 2 is lifted on top of that.

Gravity is the symmetry-breaker: it's what makes $\theta_1$ a real coordinate and not just a free choice of orientation.

8 The Lagrangian $L = T - V$

Putting it all together gives the Lagrangian as an explicit function of $(\theta_1, \theta_2, \dot{\theta}_1, \dot{\theta}_2)$:

$$L = T - V$$

$$L = \tfrac{1}{2}m_1 L_1^2\dot{\theta}_1^2 + \tfrac{1}{2}m_2\!\left[L_1^2\dot{\theta}_1^2 + L_2^2(\dot{\theta}_1+\dot{\theta}_2)^2 + 2L_1L_2\cos\theta_2\,\dot{\theta}_1(\dot{\theta}_1+\dot{\theta}_2)\right]$$

$$\;-\; m_1 g L_1\sin\theta_1 \;-\; m_2 g\,[L_1\sin\theta_1 + L_2\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2)]$$

This is the object Module 3 will feed into the Euler–Lagrange equations:

$$\frac{d}{dt}\!\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot{\theta}_i}\right) - \frac{\partial L}{\partial \theta_i} = \tau_i$$

for $i = 1, 2$, where $\tau_i$ is the generalised force (joint torque) on coordinate $\theta_i$.

You've now got the dynamics-in-waiting. Two partial derivatives, one time derivative each, and you'll have the equations of motion of the arm.
Recap — the recipe. $T_i \to (x_i, y_i) \to (\dot{x}_i, \dot{y}_i) \to v_i^2 \to T \to V \to L$. Same six steps for any serial manipulator; only the algebra grows.
Common pitfall. Students often forget to differentiate the inner angle when applying the chain rule, dropping the $(\dot{\theta}_1+\dot{\theta}_2)$ factor. That single error breaks every later step — double-check §3.
