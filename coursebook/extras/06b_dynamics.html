Lecture 03 · Module 3 · Robot Manipulator Dynamics

Lagrangian formulation for robot dynamics

From link velocities to the complete equation $\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})\ddot{\mathbf{q}} + \mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}}) + \mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q}) = \boldsymbol{\tau}$.

Lecturer. bibliosense Lecture. 03 Course. Robot Manipulator Dynamics
Today we build the equations of motion for a serial manipulator from the ground up. We start from transformation matrices you already know, take a derivative to get link velocities, sum kinetic energies into an inertia matrix $\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})$, then chase the Euler–Lagrange equation through Christoffel symbols to recover the Coriolis term $\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}})$ and the gravity term $\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q})$. We finish by working a 2-DOF planar arm out by hand — every step, no shortcuts.

0What this lecture covers

We assemble the complete robot dynamic equation $\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})\ddot{\mathbf{q}} + \mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}}) + \mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q}) = \boldsymbol{\tau}$ piece by piece, using the Lagrangian formulation.

The roadmap: link velocities via transformation matrices → kinetic energy and the inertia matrix $\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})$ → velocity-dependent Coriolis/centrifugal terms $\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}})$ via Christoffel symbols → gravitational terms $\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q})$ from potential energy → assembly, a worked 2-DOF example, and a look at computational efficiency.

By the end you should be able to derive each component systematically and recognise its physical meaning in a real manipulator.

If you remember one thing from this lecture: the Lagrangian approach is just bookkeeping — energy in, derivatives out, equations of motion fall out the bottom.
Learning targets. Velocity influence coefficients $\mathbf{U}_{ij}$, the inertia matrix $\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})$, Christoffel symbols and $\mathbf{C}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}})$, gravity terms $\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q})$, and full assembly into a usable dynamic model.

1Velocity formulation via transformation matrices

The position of a point fixed in link $i$, expressed in the base frame, is the chain product ${}^0\mathbf{r}_i = {}^0\mathbf{A}_1\,{}^1\mathbf{A}_2 \cdots {}^{i-1}\mathbf{A}_i\,{}^i\mathbf{r}_i$. Differentiating gives velocities that depend linearly on the joint rates $\dot{q}_j$.

The partial derivatives $\partial {}^0\mathbf{A}_i/\partial q_j$ have a beautifully clean form: insert a constant $4\times 4$ matrix $\mathbf{Q}_j$ (different for revolute vs. prismatic joints) into the chain at position $j$. We collect this into the velocity influence coefficient $\mathbf{U}_{ij}$:

$$\mathbf{U}_{ij} = \begin{cases} {}^0\mathbf{A}_j \mathbf{Q}_j {}^j\mathbf{A}_i & j \leq i \\ \mathbf{0} & j > i \end{cases}$$

The $\mathbf{0}$ branch encodes a physical truth: a joint can only influence links that come after it in the chain.

The $\mathbf{Q}_j$ matrix is the 'derivative of a rotation' in disguise — that's why it has just two non-zero entries.
$\mathbf{Q}_j$ for each joint type. Revolute: a $4\times 4$ skew block in the upper-left $2\times 2$. Prismatic: a single $1$ in row 3, column 4 — pure translation along the joint axis.
Joint coupling $\mathbf{U}_{ijk}$. For kinetic energy we also need the second derivative $\partial \mathbf{U}_{ij}/\partial q_k = \mathbf{U}_{ijk}$ — two $\mathbf{Q}$ insertions in the chain. This is where dynamic complexity grows quadratically with DOF.
Compact velocity expression (eq. 3.8)
⁰vᵢ = ( Σⱼ₌₁ⁱ Uᵢⱼ q̇ⱼ ) · ⁱrᵢ

Uᵢⱼ = ⁰Aⱼ · Qⱼ · ʲAᵢ      for j ≤ i
Uᵢⱼ = 0                  for j > i

2Kinetic energy and the inertia matrix $\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})$

Sum the kinetic energy of every link, write each link's KE using $\mathbf{U}_{ij}$ and the link inertia tensor $\mathbf{J}_i$, and rearrange the triple sum. Comparing with the canonical quadratic form $T = \tfrac{1}{2}\dot{\mathbf{q}}^T \mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q}) \dot{\mathbf{q}}$ we read off:

$$D_{jk}(\mathbf{q}) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \mathrm{Tr}\!\left(\mathbf{U}_{ij}\, \mathbf{J}_i\, \mathbf{U}_{ik}^{T}\right)$$

This single equation is the workhorse of Lagrangian robot dynamics.

Properties to commit to memory: $\mathbf{D}$ is symmetric (trace and transpose), positive definite (KE is positive for any non-zero motion), and configuration-dependent (the $\mathbf{U}_{ij}$ change with $\mathbf{q}$). Diagonal entries $D_{jj}$ are the effective inertia at joint $j$; off-diagonals $D_{jk}$ are inertial coupling between joints.

If $\mathbf{D}$ ever drops rank in your simulation, you have a bug — physically impossible.
Why the trace? We're using $4\times 4$ homogeneous matrices, so KE for a link emerges from $\tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{Tr}(\dot{\mathbf{T}}\mathbf{J}\dot{\mathbf{T}}^T)$. The trace collapses the matrix back to a scalar.
Algorithm 3.1 — Inertia matrix computation
Input:  q, link inertial parameters
Output: D(q)

1. D(q) ← 0_{n×n}
2. for i = 1 .. n:
     compute ⁰A₁, ⁰A₂, ..., ⁰Aᵢ
     for each (j,k) with j,k ≤ i:
         Uᵢⱼ ← ⁰Aⱼ · Qⱼ · ʲAᵢ
         Uᵢₖ ← ⁰Aₖ · Qₖ · ᵏAᵢ
         D[j,k] ← D[j,k] + Tr(Uᵢⱼ · Jᵢ · Uᵢₖᵀ)
3. enforce symmetry: D[k,j] ← D[j,k]

3Coriolis and centrifugal terms via Christoffel symbols

Plug $T = \tfrac{1}{2}\dot{\mathbf{q}}^T\mathbf{D}\dot{\mathbf{q}}$ into Euler–Lagrange. The time derivative $\frac{d}{dt}(\partial T/\partial \dot{q}_i)$ produces both the inertial term $\sum_j D_{ij}\ddot{q}_j$ and velocity-quadratic terms $\sum_{j,k}(\partial D_{ij}/\partial q_k)\dot{q}_j\dot{q}_k$. The other half $\partial T/\partial q_i$ also contributes velocity-quadratic terms. They combine into the Christoffel symbols of the first kind:

$$\Gamma_{ijk} = \tfrac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial D_{ij}}{\partial q_k} + \frac{\partial D_{ik}}{\partial q_j} - \frac{\partial D_{jk}}{\partial q_i}\right)$$

The Coriolis/centrifugal vector is $h_i = \sum_{j,k}\Gamma_{ijk}\dot{q}_j\dot{q}_k$, or in matrix form $\mathbf{h} = \mathbf{C}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}})\dot{\mathbf{q}}$. Diagonal $\dot{q}_j^2$ contributions are centrifugal; cross terms $\dot{q}_j\dot{q}_k$ are Coriolis.

A crucial property: $\dot{\mathbf{D}} - 2\mathbf{C}$ is skew-symmetric — this is the passivity property that underwrites every Lyapunov-based robot controller you'll meet later.

At high speed these terms are not a footnote — Coriolis can eat 20–40% of your actuator effort.
Centrifugal vs. Coriolis — quick rule. Centrifugal: one joint moving fast affects itself (term in $\dot{q}_j^2$). Coriolis: two joints moving simultaneously couple into a third (term in $\dot{q}_j\dot{q}_k$, $j\ne k$).
Skew-symmetry of $\dot{\mathbf{D}} - 2\mathbf{C}$. Equivalent to energy conservation: kinetic energy changes only because of work done by external torques, not because of internal coupling. Foundation for passivity-based control.
Algorithm 3.2 — Coriolis and centrifugal terms
Input:  q, q̇, D(q)
Output: h(q, q̇)

1. compute ∂D[j,k]/∂qᵢ  for all i,j,k
2. Γᵢⱼₖ ← ½ ( ∂D[i,j]/∂qₖ + ∂D[i,k]/∂qⱼ − ∂D[j,k]/∂qᵢ )
3. hᵢ   ← Σⱼ Σₖ  Γᵢⱼₖ · q̇ⱼ · q̇ₖ

4Gravitational terms from potential energy

Total potential energy is just $V = \sum_i m_i \mathbf{g}^T {}^0\mathbf{r}_{ci}$. The gravity term in the dynamic equation is $c_i(\mathbf{q}) = \partial V/\partial q_i$, and using the same $\mathbf{U}_{ji}$ machinery we already have:

$$c_i(\mathbf{q}) = \sum_{j=i}^{n} m_j \mathbf{g}^T \mathbf{U}_{ji}\, {}^j\mathbf{r}_{cj}$$

Notice the lower limit $j=i$: joint $i$ only has to support links outboard of itself (links $i, i+1, \dots, n$).

Gravity scales with configuration as $\sim \sin\theta$ for a link rotating in a vertical plane — zero when vertical, maximum when horizontal. For large industrial arms, gravity torques can be 10–50× larger than dynamic torques during normal operation; this is why gravity compensation is the first feedforward term any controller adds.

Gravity is the cheap term: configuration only, no velocities, easy to precompute or table-lookup.
Static torque intuition. Vertical link → moment arm is zero → minimal torque. Horizontal link → full moment arm → maximum torque. Everything between scales with $\sin\theta$.
Algorithm 3.3 — Gravity terms
Input:  q, link masses mⱼ, COM positions ʲr_cⱼ
Output: c(q)

1. c(q) ← 0_n
2. for i = 1 .. n:
     for j = i .. n:
         compute Uⱼᵢ
         c[i] ← c[i] + mⱼ · gᵀ · Uⱼᵢ · ʲr_cⱼ

5Assembling the complete dynamic equation

Stacking the three contributions gives the canonical robot equation of motion:

$$\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})\ddot{\mathbf{q}} + \mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}}) + \mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q}) = \boldsymbol{\tau} \tag{3.26}$$

This is the entire Lagrangian story in one line. Each term is physically distinct: $\mathbf{D}\ddot{\mathbf{q}}$ is inertial, $\mathbf{h}$ is velocity-dependent (Coriolis + centrifugal), $\mathbf{c}$ is static (gravity), and $\boldsymbol{\tau}$ is what your motors deliver.

Three useful rearrangements:

  • Forward dynamics (for simulation): $\ddot{\mathbf{q}} = \mathbf{D}^{-1}[\boldsymbol{\tau} - \mathbf{h} - \mathbf{c}]$.
  • State-space form: stack $[\mathbf{q}; \dot{\mathbf{q}}]$ for ODE solvers and controller design.
  • Linearisation about $(\mathbf{q}_0, \dot{\mathbf{q}}_0)$: gives an LTI system $\mathbf{D}_0\Delta\ddot{\mathbf{q}} + \mathbf{C}_0\Delta\dot{\mathbf{q}} + \mathbf{K}_0\Delta\mathbf{q} = \Delta\boldsymbol{\tau}$, useful for stability analysis.
Inverse dynamics gives you torques from a desired motion. Forward dynamics gives you motion from applied torques. Same equation, opposite directions.
Properties to lean on. $\mathbf{D} = \mathbf{D}^T$, $\mathbf{D} \succ 0$, $\dot{\mathbf{D}} - 2\mathbf{C}$ skew-symmetric, all terms bounded for finite $(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}})$. These four facts power most stability proofs in robot control.

6Worked example: 2-DOF planar arm

Two uniform-rod links, revolute joints, no friction; $q_1=\theta_1$ from horizontal, $q_2=\theta_2$ relative to link 1. We work all seven steps: positions → velocities → kinetic energy → potential energy → Coriolis → gravity → assembled equation.

Inertia matrix (eqs. 3.39–3.41):

$$D_{11} = m_1\tfrac{L_1^2}{4} + I_1 + m_2\!\left[L_1^2 + \tfrac{L_2^2}{4} + L_1L_2\cos\theta_2\right] + I_2$$

$$D_{12} = D_{21} = m_2\!\left[\tfrac{L_2^2}{4} + \tfrac{L_1L_2}{2}\cos\theta_2\right] + I_2$$

$$D_{22} = m_2\tfrac{L_2^2}{4} + I_2$$

Coriolis/centrifugal (eqs. 3.45–3.46):

$$h_1 = -m_2 L_1 L_2 \sin\theta_2\,\dot\theta_1\dot\theta_2 - \tfrac{m_2 L_1 L_2 \sin\theta_2}{2}\dot\theta_2^2$$

$$h_2 = \tfrac{m_2 L_1 L_2 \sin\theta_2}{2}\dot\theta_1^2$$

Gravity (eqs. 3.47–3.48):

$$c_1 = m_1 g \tfrac{L_1}{2}\cos\theta_1 + m_2 g L_1 \cos\theta_1 + m_2 g \tfrac{L_2}{2}\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2)$$

$$c_2 = m_2 g \tfrac{L_2}{2}\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2)$$

Observe: when $\theta_2 = 90°$ the inertial coupling $D_{12}$ loses its $\cos\theta_2$ contribution and the Coriolis terms peak ($\sin\theta_2 = 1$). Even two links exhibit the full menagerie — nonlinearity, configuration dependence, joint coupling.

Do this derivation by hand at least once. Once you've felt the algebra grind through $\mathbf{v}_{c2}^T\mathbf{v}_{c2}$, you'll trust the symbolic toolboxes that do it for you later.
Watch the cross terms. Expanding $\mathbf{v}_{c2}^T\mathbf{v}_{c2}$ directly from grouped derivatives gives a $\dot\theta_1\dot\theta_2$ coefficient of $L_1 L_2 \cos\theta_2 + \tfrac{L_2^2}{2}$. Don't double-count when expanding $(\dot\theta_1+\dot\theta_2)^2$.
Decoupling configuration. $\theta_2 = 90°$: inertia coupling vanishes via $\cos\theta_2 = 0$, Coriolis maximises via $\sin\theta_2 = 1$. A single posture, two opposite phenomena.
Final 2-DOF dynamic equation (eq. 3.49)
[ D₁₁  D₁₂ ] [ θ̈₁ ]   [ h₁ ]   [ c₁ ]   [ τ₁ ]
[ D₂₁  D₂₂ ] [ θ̈₂ ] + [ h₂ ] + [ c₂ ] = [ τ₂ ]

with all D, h, c terms as defined in eqs. 3.39–3.48.

7Computational considerations

Computational complexity, naive vs. smart:

  • Direct Lagrangian: inertia $O(n^4)$, Coriolis $O(n^3)$, gravity $O(n^2)$ → $O(n^4)$ total.
  • Recursive Newton–Euler: inverse dynamics in $O(n)$, forward dynamics in $O(n^3)$ (matrix inversion dominates).

For real-time control at kHz rates, the $n$ in the exponent matters. Industrial controllers (KUKA, ABB, FANUC) lean heavily on symbolic preprocessing: derive equations symbolically once per robot, generate optimised C/C++, compile, deploy. Generic-algorithm overhead disappears.

Numerical hygiene for $\mathbf{D}^{-1}$: prefer LU decomposition over direct inversion; better still, exploit symmetry/positive-definiteness with Cholesky. Near singularities, regularise the diagonal or fall back to an SVD-based pseudoinverse.

Real-time robotics is where elegant math meets the wall clock — and the wall clock always wins.
Pick the right algorithm for the job. Control loop with desired trajectory? You want inverse dynamics ($O(n)$ recursive Newton–Euler). Physics simulator? You need forward dynamics — pay the $O(n^3)$ but use Cholesky.
Singularities. Near kinematic singularities $\mathbf{D}$ stays well-conditioned but the task-space mapping degenerates. Different problem — handle in the controller, not in the dynamics solver.
