Lecture 02 · Module 2 · Robot Manipulator Dynamics

Energy-Based Approach to Robot Dynamics

Lagrangian mechanics as the systematic route from kinetic and potential energy to the standard robot dynamic equation.

Lecturer. bibliosense Lecture. 02
In this module we step away from free-body diagrams and constraint bookkeeping, and let energy do the heavy lifting. Once we can write down the kinetic and potential energy of a multi-link arm, the Euler–Lagrange machine cranks out the equations of motion for us — automatically dropping the constraint forces, naturally exposing inertial coupling, Coriolis effects and gravity, and producing the matrix form $\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})\ddot{\mathbf{q}} + \mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}}) + \mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q}) = \boldsymbol{\tau}$ that every modern controller is built on.

0 What this lecture covers

This module builds the energy-based foundation for robot dynamics. We compare force methods (Newton–Euler) with energy methods (Lagrangian), then develop kinetic and potential energy expressions for multi-link manipulators, introduce generalized coordinates and the Lagrangian $L = T - V$, and apply the Euler–Lagrange equations to derive the standard robot equation of motion.

Along the way we look at velocity kinematics — how every joint downstream contributes to a link's linear and angular velocity — and we see how those expressions package neatly into the inertia matrix $\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})$, the Coriolis/centrifugal term $\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}})$, and the gravity term $\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q})$.

By the end you should be able to take any robot configuration and, in a systematic way, write down its equations of motion from energy alone.

If you have ever drawn ten free-body diagrams for a 3-DOF arm and lost a sign somewhere, this module is your peace offering.
Learning objectives.Master energy fundamentals in multi-link systems, the Lagrangian framework, generalized coordinates, the Euler–Lagrange equations, and the systematic derivation of $\mathbf{D}\ddot{\mathbf{q}} + \mathbf{h} + \mathbf{c} = \boldsymbol{\tau}$.

1 Why energy methods beat force methods

Newton–Euler and Lagrangian approaches are mathematically equivalent — they yield the same equations — but the path is wildly different. For a single pendulum, free-body diagrams are intuitive. For a 3-DOF arm with coupling between every joint, that intuition collapses under bookkeeping.

The fundamental win of the energy approach is that energy is scalar. Instead of tracking vector forces, constraint reactions and inter-link interactions, you write down two scalar functions ($T$ and $V$) and let the Euler–Lagrange machinery do the rest. Constraint forces automatically drop out of the final equations.

This is what makes the energy method the standard tool for systematic, automatable, error-resistant modelling of complex robots.

Force methods scale badly. Energy methods scale.
Newton–Euler: what bites you.Constraint forces at every joint, vector bookkeeping across coordinate frames, inter-link interaction forces, and easy sign errors in long kinematic chains.
Lagrangian: what helps you.Scalar energies, automatic constraint elimination, a fixed step-by-step procedure, and freedom to use whatever generalized coordinates make life easiest.
3-DOF arm: side-by-side.Force route: 6 equations per link + constraint elimination across 3 links. Energy route: write $T$ and $V$, form $L = T - V$, turn the Euler–Lagrange crank. Same answer, far less pain.

2 Kinetic and potential energy in multi-link systems

Robotic kinetic energy is more than $\tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$. Every link carries both translational kinetic energy (motion of its centre of mass) and rotational kinetic energy (spin about its CoM). Worse, velocities are coupled through the kinematic chain: when joint 1 moves, every downstream link moves with it.

Potential energy is gentler — it depends only on configuration, not velocity. In a uniform gravitational field it is simply the sum of $m_i \mathbf{g}^T \mathbf{r}_i$ over all links. But it is strongly configuration-dependent: an extended horizontal arm stores far more potential energy than the same arm folded vertically, and that difference is exactly the gravitational torque the actuators must hold up.

Every joint downstream is along for the ride — and they all show up in the kinetic energy.
Velocity coupling.$\mathbf{v}_i$ and $\boldsymbol{\omega}_i$ both depend on every joint between link $i$ and the base. That coupling is the source of the inertial cross-terms later.
Why potential energy is easy.It depends only on position. A clever choice of reference height can eliminate constants and simplify the Euler–Lagrange derivatives.
Total kinetic energy of an n-link manipulator
T = (1/2) Σᵢ [ mᵢ vᵢᵀ vᵢ + ωᵢᵀ Iᵢ ωᵢ ]

mᵢ : mass of link i
vᵢ : linear velocity of link i's CoM
ωᵢ : angular velocity of link i
Iᵢ : inertia tensor of link i about its CoM
Total gravitational potential energy
V = Σᵢ mᵢ gᵀ rᵢ

g  : gravitational acceleration vector (e.g. [0, 0, -9.81]ᵀ)
rᵢ : position of link i's CoM

3 Lagrangian mechanics: generalized coordinates and $L = T - V$

Lagrangian mechanics builds on generalized coordinates $\mathbf{q}$ — any independent set of parameters that pins down the configuration. For robots, the joint variables themselves are the natural choice: revolute angles, prismatic displacements. They are minimal (no redundant DOFs), they automatically respect the kinematic constraints of the linkage, and they map one-to-one to the actuators we actually command.

The Lagrangian is then the scalar $L(\mathbf{q}, \dot{\mathbf{q}}, t) = T(\mathbf{q}, \dot{\mathbf{q}}) - V(\mathbf{q}).$ Physically, it is the excess of kinetic energy over potential energy. The principle of least action tells us that the system evolves along trajectories that extremize $\int L \, dt$, and that single statement is enough to generate every equation of motion we need.

Pick joint variables, write one scalar, and you have everything.
Why generalized coordinates win.Minimal representation, automatic constraint satisfaction, and direct correspondence to actuator commands — joint angles are simultaneously the math-friendly and the engineering-friendly choice.
The Lagrangian function
L(q, q̇, t) = T(q, q̇) − V(q)

q  : generalized coordinates (joint variables)
q̇  : generalized velocities
T  : total kinetic energy
V  : total potential energy

4 The Euler–Lagrange equations

For a system with $n$ degrees of freedom, the equations of motion are $\frac{d}{dt}\!\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot{q}_i}\right) - \frac{\partial L}{\partial q_i} = \tau_i, \qquad i = 1, \ldots, n.$ Here $\tau_i$ is the generalized force conjugate to coordinate $q_i$ — for a revolute joint it is a torque, for a prismatic joint it is a force.

The procedure is mechanical: write $T$ and $V$ in terms of $\mathbf{q}$ and $\dot{\mathbf{q}}$, form $L$, take the prescribed partials, and simplify. The systematic nature is what makes the approach so robust — you can verify it line by line, and you can automate it in symbolic computation tools.

It's a recipe. Follow the steps. Don't improvise.
The five-step recipe.1) write $T$ and $V$ in $(\mathbf{q}, \dot{\mathbf{q}})$, 2) form $L = T - V$, 3) compute $\partial L/\partial \dot{q}_i$ and $\partial L/\partial q_i$, 4) substitute into Euler–Lagrange, 5) simplify.
Euler–Lagrange equations
d/dt ( ∂L/∂q̇ᵢ ) − ∂L/∂qᵢ = τᵢ      for i = 1, …, n

∂L/∂q̇ᵢ : partial wrt generalized velocity i
∂L/∂qᵢ  : partial wrt generalized coordinate i
τᵢ      : generalized force on coordinate i (joint torque/force)

5 Worked example: applying Euler–Lagrange to a 2-DOF planar arm

Take a 2-DOF planar arm with revolute joints, link lengths $L_1, L_2$, masses $m_1, m_2$ and inertias $I_1, I_2$.

Step 1 — generalized coordinates. Choose $q_1 = \theta_1$ (first joint angle) and $q_2 = \theta_2$ (second joint angle, relative to link 1).

Step 2 — kinetic energy. Link 1 rotates about the base with angular velocity $\dot{\theta}_1$. Link 2's motion is coupled: its CoM moves due to both $\dot{\theta}_1$ and $\dot{\theta}_2$, and its angular velocity is $\dot{\theta}_1 + \dot{\theta}_2$. The total $T$ ends up a quadratic in $(\dot{\theta}_1, \dot{\theta}_2)$ with coupling terms.

Step 3 — potential energy. Link 1's CoM height depends on $\theta_1$; link 2's height depends on both $\theta_1$ and $\theta_2$.

Step 4 — turn the crank. Apply Euler–Lagrange to each $q_i$. Out come coupled, nonlinear ODEs that capture inertial coupling, Coriolis and centrifugal effects, and gravitational loading.

Even this minimal example has nontrivial cross-terms — which is exactly why the systematic Lagrangian recipe is worth its weight in gold.

Two joints, two equations, four physical phenomena packed inside.
What the resulting ODEs contain.Inertial coupling between joints, Coriolis and centrifugal velocity-product terms, and gravitational loading — all separated cleanly by the Lagrangian derivation.

6 Velocity kinematics for energy calculations

To compute $T$ you need each link's linear and angular velocity in terms of joint velocities. The chain rule gives the position-to-velocity link: $\mathbf{v}_i = \frac{\partial \mathbf{r}_i}{\partial q_1}\dot{q}_1 + \cdots + \frac{\partial \mathbf{r}_i}{\partial q_i}\dot{q}_i = \mathbf{J}_{vi}(\mathbf{q})\,\dot{\mathbf{q}},$ where $\mathbf{J}_{vi}$ is the velocity Jacobian for link $i$'s CoM.

For angular velocities, revolute joints each contribute their joint rate to every downstream link, while prismatic joints contribute zero. So in a planar 3R arm: $\boldsymbol{\omega}_1 = \dot{q}_1$, $\boldsymbol{\omega}_2 = \dot{q}_1 + \dot{q}_2$, $\boldsymbol{\omega}_3 = \dot{q}_1 + \dot{q}_2 + \dot{q}_3$.

This cumulative coupling is the source of the off-diagonal terms in the inertia matrix.

Downstream links inherit everything that happens upstream of them. That is the whole story of robot velocity kinematics.
Planar 3R angular velocities.$\boldsymbol{\omega}_1 = \dot{q}_1$; $\boldsymbol{\omega}_2 = \dot{q}_1 + \dot{q}_2$; $\boldsymbol{\omega}_3 = \dot{q}_1 + \dot{q}_2 + \dot{q}_3$. Each link inherits the rates of every joint upstream of it.
Linear velocity via the velocity Jacobian
vᵢ = (∂rᵢ/∂q₁) q̇₁ + (∂rᵢ/∂q₂) q̇₂ + … + (∂rᵢ/∂qᵢ) q̇ᵢ
   = J_{vi}(q) · q̇

7 Kinetic energy as a quadratic form: the inertia matrix $\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})$

When every $\mathbf{v}_i$ and $\boldsymbol{\omega}_i$ is linear in $\dot{\mathbf{q}}$, the kinetic energy becomes a quadratic form in the joint velocities: $T = \tfrac{1}{2}\,\dot{\mathbf{q}}^T \mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})\,\dot{\mathbf{q}}.$ The matrix $\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})$ is the inertia matrix. Its diagonal entries $D_{ii}$ are the effective inertia seen by joint $i$ when only joint $i$ moves; its off-diagonal entries $D_{ij}$ encode inertial coupling between joints $i$ and $j$.

Two properties make $\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})$ especially well-behaved: it is symmetric and positive definite for every physically reasonable configuration. That guarantees real-valued kinetic energy, well-posed equations of motion, and a stable foundation for control design. But it is configuration-dependent — as the robot moves, $\mathbf{D}$ changes, and that variation is what eventually generates the Coriolis and centrifugal terms.

Symmetric, positive definite, configuration-dependent. Memorise that triplet.
Reading $\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})$.Diagonal: effective inertia of each joint in isolation. Off-diagonal: coupling between joints. Configuration dependence: posture changes how heavy each joint feels.
Inertia matrix entries
D_{ij}(q) = Σₖ [ mₖ (∂rₖ/∂qᵢ)ᵀ (∂rₖ/∂qⱼ)
              + (∂ωₖ/∂q̇ᵢ)ᵀ Iₖ (∂ωₖ/∂q̇ⱼ) ]

T = (1/2) q̇ᵀ D(q) q̇

8 From the Lagrangian to the standard robot equation

Now we turn the Euler–Lagrange crank on $L = \tfrac{1}{2}\dot{\mathbf{q}}^T \mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})\dot{\mathbf{q}} - V(\mathbf{q})$.

Differentiating $T$ with respect to $\dot{q}_i$ gives $\sum_j D_{ij}\dot{q}_j$, and the time derivative produces both an inertial term $\sum_j D_{ij}\ddot{q}_j$ and velocity-product terms involving $\partial D_{ij}/\partial q_k$. Subtracting $\partial T/\partial q_i$ leaves the symmetrized Christoffel-style combination that is the Coriolis/centrifugal term. Finally $\partial V/\partial q_i$ contributes the gravity term.

Collecting everything in matrix form gives the standard robot dynamic equation: $\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})\ddot{\mathbf{q}} + \mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}}) + \mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q}) = \boldsymbol{\tau}.$

Three terms, three physical meanings, one equation that runs every modern robot.
$\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})$ — inertia.From $T$. Symmetric, positive definite, configuration-dependent. Diagonal = effective joint inertia; off-diagonal = inertial coupling.
$\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}})$ — Coriolis & centrifugal.Velocity-product forces from $\partial D_{ij}/\partial q_k$. Components: $h_{ijk} = \tfrac{1}{2}(\partial D_{ij}/\partial q_k + \partial D_{ik}/\partial q_j - \partial D_{jk}/\partial q_i)$.
$\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q})$ — gravity.From $V$. $c_i = \partial V/\partial q_i$. Configuration-dependent gravitational torques the actuators must hold.
Euler–Lagrange applied to the kinetic energy
∂T/∂q̇ᵢ          = Σⱼ D_{ij}(q) q̇ⱼ

d/dt(∂T/∂q̇ᵢ)    = Σⱼ D_{ij} q̈ⱼ + Σⱼ Σₖ (∂D_{ij}/∂qₖ) q̇ₖ q̇ⱼ

∂T/∂qᵢ           = (1/2) Σⱼ Σₖ (∂D_{jk}/∂qᵢ) q̇ⱼ q̇ₖ
Standard robot dynamic equation
D(q) q̈ + h(q, q̇) + c(q) = τ

D(q)     : inertia matrix
h(q, q̇) : Coriolis/centrifugal vector
c(q)     : gravity vector
τ        : joint torques/forces

9 Why the energy approach wins, and where it shows up in industry

The Lagrangian route gives us five compounding wins: a systematic procedure that works for any robot; physical insight because each term ($\mathbf{D}$, $\mathbf{h}$, $\mathbf{c}$) means something concrete; computational structure that algorithms can exploit; error reduction because constraint forces and most sign traps disappear; and automation, because the procedure runs cleanly through symbolic computation.

This is not academic. Modern industrial controllers compute $\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})$, $\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}})$ and $\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q})$ at 1 kHz or faster, and feed them into trajectory optimization, collision detection and predictive maintenance. A reported automotive welding-cell deployment moved from PID with $\pm 2\,\mathrm{mm}$ accuracy and 45 s cycle time to dynamic-model control with $\pm 0.2\,\mathrm{mm}$ accuracy and 28 s cycle time — a 35 % production speed-up, a 10× accuracy improvement, and 25 % less energy used. That is the practical payoff of the equation we just derived.

This is the equation that runs in the cabinet next to the robot.
