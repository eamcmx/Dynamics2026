The Scalar Triple Product

Mixing the dot and the cross to measure volume — and to test when three vectors collapse onto a plane.

Lecturer. bibliosense Lecture. 06
Today we braid together the two products you already know. Take the cross of two vectors, then dot the result with a third — what falls out is a single number that simultaneously tells you the signed volume of the parallelepiped they span and whether the three vectors lie flat in a common plane. We will derive the determinant formula, work through its symmetries, and then put it to work on tetrahedra: volumes, heights, and coplanarity tests.

0What this lecture covers

We define the scalar triple product $\mathbf{a}\cdot(\mathbf{b}\times\mathbf{c})$, prove it equals a $3\times 3$ determinant of the components, and read off its geometric meaning as the volume of the parallelepiped spanned by $\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b},\mathbf{c}$.

From that single object we get a clean coplanarity test, a tidy set of cyclic-permutation rules, and a fast way to compute volumes and heights of tetrahedra. Three worked examples close the lecture.

Two products, one number — and that number is a volume. Keep that picture in mind and the algebra stops feeling arbitrary.
Where we are. This builds directly on the dot product (Lecture 04) and the vector product (Lecture 05). If $\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b}$ is fuzzy, revisit §5.5 first.

1Definition — combining dot and cross

Given three vectors $\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b}, \mathbf{c}$, the scalar triple product (also called the mixed product) is

$[\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b},\mathbf{c}] \;=\; \mathbf{a}\cdot(\mathbf{b}\times\mathbf{c}).$

Notice the order: cross first, then dot. The cross product produces a vector; dotting it with $\mathbf{a}$ collapses everything down to a single scalar. That scalar carries a surprising amount of geometric information.

Mind the operation order. $(\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b})\times\mathbf{c}$ is meaningless — you can't cross a scalar with a vector. The grouping in $\mathbf{a}\cdot(\mathbf{b}\times\mathbf{c})$ is the only one that type-checks.

2The determinant formula

Theorem. In a rectangular coordinate system with $\mathbf{a}=\{a_x,a_y,a_z\}$, $\mathbf{b}=\{b_x,b_y,b_z\}$, $\mathbf{c}=\{c_x,c_y,c_z\}$,

$\mathbf{a}\cdot(\mathbf{b}\times\mathbf{c}) \;=\; \begin{vmatrix} a_x & a_y & a_z \\ b_x & b_y & b_z \\ c_x & c_y & c_z \end{vmatrix}. \qquad (9)$

Proof sketch. Expanding the cross product

$\mathbf{b}\times\mathbf{c} = (b_y c_z - b_z c_y)\mathbf{i} - (b_x c_z - b_z c_x)\mathbf{j} + (b_x c_y - b_y c_x)\mathbf{k},$

then dotting with $\mathbf{a} = a_x\mathbf{i} + a_y\mathbf{j} + a_z\mathbf{k}$, gives exactly the cofactor expansion of the $3\times 3$ determinant along the first row. So the algebra and the determinant agree term-for-term.

This is the single most useful identity in the chapter — almost every problem reduces to evaluating one of these determinants.
Determinant template
          | a_x  a_y  a_z |
 a·(b×c) = | b_x  b_y  b_z |
          | c_x  c_y  c_z |

3Geometric meaning — volume of a parallelepiped

Place the three vectors at a common origin and complete the parallelepiped they span. Then

$\bigl|\,\mathbf{a}\cdot(\mathbf{b}\times\mathbf{c})\,\bigr| \;=\; V_{\text{parallelepiped}}.$

Why. Volume = (area of base) $\times$ (height). The vector $\mathbf{b}\times\mathbf{c}$ is normal to the base parallelogram, and its length $|\mathbf{b}\times\mathbf{c}|$ equals that base area. Dotting with $\mathbf{a}$ projects $\mathbf{a}$ onto that normal:

$\mathbf{a}\cdot(\mathbf{b}\times\mathbf{c}) = |\mathbf{a}|\,|\mathbf{b}\times\mathbf{c}|\cos\varphi,$

and $|\mathbf{a}|\cos\varphi$ is precisely the height of the parallelepiped. The sign of the triple product encodes orientation (right-handed vs left-handed); the absolute value gives the volume.

The cross product builds the floor; the dot product measures how tall the box stands above it.
Sign = handedness. Positive triple product → right-handed triple. Negative → left-handed. Zero → flat.

4Coplanarity — the flat-box test

If the parallelepiped has zero volume, it is flat — the three vectors lie in a common plane.

Corollary 1. If $\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b},\mathbf{c}$ are coplanar, then $\mathbf{a}\cdot(\mathbf{b}\times\mathbf{c}) = 0$.

Corollary 2. Four points $A,B,C,D$ lie in the same plane iff

$\overrightarrow{AB}\cdot(\overrightarrow{AC}\times\overrightarrow{AD}) = 0.$

This is the workhorse coplanarity test: pick any vertex as the anchor, form three edge-vectors out of it, and evaluate one determinant.

Equivalent statements. For non-zero $\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b},\mathbf{c}$: triple product = 0 ⟺ vectors are linearly dependent ⟺ vectors are coplanar.

5Algebraic properties — cyclic symmetry

From the symmetry of the dot product and the row-swap rules of determinants, the triple product has a clean structure.

Order of dot and cross is irrelevant. $\mathbf{a}\cdot(\mathbf{b}\times\mathbf{c}) = (\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b})\cdot\mathbf{c}$. We therefore drop the parentheses and write simply $\mathbf{abc}$.

Cyclic permutations preserve the value:

$\mathbf{abc} \;=\; \mathbf{cab} \;=\; \mathbf{bca}.$

Swapping any two flips the sign:

$\mathbf{abc} \;=\; -\,\mathbf{bac} \;=\; -\,\mathbf{acb}.$

These mirror exactly the row-swap behaviour of a $3\times 3$ determinant — which is, of course, the same object.

Cyclic = same. Anti-cyclic = sign flip. The notation $\mathbf{abc}$ keeps the algebra honest.

6Linear dependence test

By the theorem on linearly dependent vectors, any three linearly dependent vectors are coplanar — so they fit inside a flat (zero-volume) parallelepiped.

The triple product of non-zero vectors equals zero if and only if the vectors are linearly dependent.

This turns a question about vector spaces (is $\{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b},\mathbf{c}\}$ a basis?) into a question about a single number (is the determinant zero?). It is the cheapest basis check you have for $\mathbb{R}^3$.

Useful corollary. Three vectors form a basis of $\mathbb{R}^3$ ⟺ their triple product is non-zero.

7Example 1 — Are four points coplanar?

Problem. Determine whether the points $A(1,-2,2)$, $B(3,3,4)$, $C(2,-2,10)$, and $D(0,2,2)$ lie on the same plane.

Solution. Anchor at $A$ and form three edge-vectors:

$\overrightarrow{AB} = \{4,1,2\}, \quad \overrightarrow{AC} = \{3,4,8\}, \quad \overrightarrow{AD} = \{1,0,0\}.$

Compute the triple product (expand along the third row, which has two zeros):

$\begin{vmatrix} 4 & 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 & 8 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \end{vmatrix} = 1\cdot\begin{vmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 4 & 8 \end{vmatrix} = 1\cdot(8-8) = 0.$

The triple product vanishes, so the three edge-vectors are coplanar — the four points lie in a common plane.

Pick the row with the most zeros to expand along. Less arithmetic, fewer sign mistakes.

8Example 2 — Volume of a tetrahedron

Problem. Find the volume $V$ of the tetrahedron with vertices $A(1,0,2)$, $B(-3,1,4)$, $C(1,5,2)$, $D(4,4,4)$.

Strategy. Build the parallelepiped on the same three edges from $A$. Its volume is $|\mathbf{abc}|$, and the tetrahedron is exactly one-sixth of that:

$V = \tfrac{1}{6}\,V_{\text{p}} = \tfrac{1}{6}\bigl|\,\overrightarrow{AB}\cdot(\overrightarrow{AC}\times\overrightarrow{AD})\,\bigr|.$

With $\overrightarrow{AB} = \{-2,1,2\}$, $\overrightarrow{AC} = \{0,5,0\}$, $\overrightarrow{AD} = \{3,4,2\}$, expand along the second row (only the middle entry survives):

$\begin{vmatrix} -2 & 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 5 & 0 \\ 3 & 4 & 2 \end{vmatrix} = 5\cdot\begin{vmatrix} -2 & 2 \\ 3 & 2 \end{vmatrix} = 5\cdot(-4-6) = -10.$

Therefore

$V = \dfrac{|-10|}{6} = \dfrac{5}{3}.$

The factor $\tfrac{1}{6}$ is the only thing separating tetrahedra from parallelepipeds — memorise it once and never derive it again.
Tetrahedron volume formula. $V_{\text{tet}} = \tfrac{1}{6}\bigl|\overrightarrow{AB}\cdot(\overrightarrow{AC}\times\overrightarrow{AD})\bigr|$ — anchor at any one vertex.

9Example 3 — Height from a vertex to a face

Problem. For the tetrahedron of Example 2, find the height $h$ dropped from $D$ to the base $\triangle ABC$.

Strategy. Use $V = \tfrac{1}{3} S\, h$, so $h = 3V/S$. We already have $V = 5/3$; we still need the area $S$ of $\triangle ABC$.

Using the cross-product area formula

$S = \tfrac{1}{2}\,\bigl|\overrightarrow{AB}\times\overrightarrow{AC}\bigr|,$

with $\overrightarrow{AB}=\{-2,1,2\}$, $\overrightarrow{AC}=\{0,5,0\}$:

$\overrightarrow{AB}\times\overrightarrow{AC} = -10\,\mathbf{i} - 10\,\mathbf{k}, \qquad \bigl|\overrightarrow{AB}\times\overrightarrow{AC}\bigr| = 10\sqrt{2}.$

So $S = 5\sqrt{2}$ and

$h \;=\; \dfrac{3V}{S} \;=\; \dfrac{3\cdot 5/3}{5\sqrt{2}} \;=\; \dfrac{5}{5\sqrt{2}} \;=\; \dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}.$

Volume from the triple product, area from the cross product, height from a one-line ratio. Three lectures' worth of machinery, all reused.
Pattern to remember. Whenever you see height from a vertex to a face, reach for $h = 3V/S$. The triple product gives $V$, the cross product gives $S$.
