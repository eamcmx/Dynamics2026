Lecture 07 · Module 2 · Robot Dynamics

Worked example — 2-link planar arm with tip masses

From transformation matrices to the Lagrangian, one careful step at a time.

Lecturer. bibliosense Lecture. 07
Today we slow down and walk a single mechanism end-to-end: a planar two-link arm with point masses $m_1$ and $m_2$ sitting at the tips of the links. We will start with the homogeneous transformation matrices, read positions straight off them, differentiate to get velocities, square and tidy to get $v^2$, and finally assemble the kinetic energy $T$, the potential $V$, and the Lagrangian $L = T - V$. By the end you should see why this scaffolding is what makes Module 3's Euler–Lagrange equations feel almost mechanical.

0What this lecture covers

A complete worked solution for the 2-link planar robot arm with concentrated tip masses $m_1$ at the end of link 1 and $m_2$ at the end of link 2. We move methodically from geometry to energy:

  1. Write the homogeneous transformations $T_1$ and $T_2$.
  2. Read off the tip positions $(x_1,y_1)$ and $(x_2,y_2)$.
  3. Differentiate to get $\dot{x}, \dot{y}$ for each mass.
  4. Form $v_1^2$ and $v_2^2$ and simplify with the Pythagorean and angle-difference identities.
  5. Assemble the kinetic energy $T$, the potential energy $V$, and the Lagrangian $L = T - V$.

No new physics — just the careful bookkeeping that the Euler–Lagrange step in Module 3 will rest on.

Setup. Two rigid links of lengths $L_1, L_2$ in a vertical plane; joint angles $\theta_1$ (shoulder) and $\theta_2$ (elbow, measured relative to link 1). Point masses $m_1, m_2$ at the distal ends. Gravity $g$ acts in $-y$.
Why tip masses? Concentrating mass at the link tips removes the moment-of-inertia integral for now. We will relax this assumption in Module 3 and recover the full inertial coupling.
Treat this as a template. Every Lagrangian problem in this course follows exactly these seven moves — only the geometry changes.

1Step 1 — Homogeneous transformations for each link

Each link contributes a planar rigid-body transform: a rotation by its joint angle followed by a translation along its own length. Composed from the base, link 1 gives

$$T_1 = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta_1 & -\sin\theta_1 & L_1\cos\theta_1 \\ \sin\theta_1 & \cos\theta_1 & L_1\sin\theta_1 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

and link 2, expressed in the base frame, accumulates both rotations:

$$T_2 = \begin{bmatrix} \cos(\theta_1+\theta_2) & -\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2) & L_1\cos\theta_1 + L_2\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2) \\ \sin(\theta_1+\theta_2) & \cos(\theta_1+\theta_2) & L_1\sin\theta_1 + L_2\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2) \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

The last column of each matrix is exactly the position of the link's tip in world coordinates — that is the only piece we will need from here on.

Reading positions. In a planar homogeneous transform the third column $(p_x, p_y, 1)^T$ is the origin of that link's frame in the base frame — i.e. the tip position.
The transformation matrices are an over-precise tool for this problem, but they are the right tool: this is the same structure you will reuse for 6-DOF arms later.

2Step 2 — Tip positions in the base frame

Reading the last column of each transform:

Mass $m_1$ (end of link 1):

$$x_1 = L_1\cos\theta_1, \qquad y_1 = L_1\sin\theta_1$$

Mass $m_2$ (end of link 2):

$$x_2 = L_1\cos\theta_1 + L_2\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2)$$

$$y_2 = L_1\sin\theta_1 + L_2\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2)$$

These are the generalized coordinate outputs: each tip's Cartesian position written purely in terms of the joint angles $\theta_1, \theta_2$ and the constant link lengths.

3Step 3 — Velocities by the chain rule

Differentiate each position with respect to time, remembering that $\theta_1$ and $\theta_2$ are functions of $t$ and that $\frac{d}{dt}\cos\theta = -\sin\theta\,\dot\theta$.

Velocity of $m_1$:

$$\dot{x}_1 = -L_1\sin\theta_1\,\dot\theta_1, \qquad \dot{y}_1 = L_1\cos\theta_1\,\dot\theta_1$$

Velocity of $m_2$ — the elbow term picks up the sum $(\dot\theta_1+\dot\theta_2)$ because angle 2 is measured relative to link 1:

$$\dot{x}_2 = -L_1\sin\theta_1\,\dot\theta_1 - L_2\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2)\,(\dot\theta_1+\dot\theta_2)$$

$$\dot{y}_2 = L_1\cos\theta_1\,\dot\theta_1 + L_2\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2)\,(\dot\theta_1+\dot\theta_2)$$

Common slip. Forgetting that link 2's orientation in the world frame is $\theta_1+\theta_2$, not $\theta_2$. Its angular velocity is therefore the sum of joint rates.
Watch the parenthesised $(\dot\theta_1+\dot\theta_2)$ — that single grouping is the most common place students lose a term.

4Step 4 — Squared speeds $v_1^2$ and $v_2^2$

Add $\dot x^2 + \dot y^2$ for each mass and use $\sin^2\alpha + \cos^2\alpha = 1$ liberally.

For $m_1$ — clean and immediate:

$$v_1^2 = \dot{x}_1^2 + \dot{y}_1^2 = L_1^2\dot\theta_1^2\big(\sin^2\theta_1 + \cos^2\theta_1\big) = L_1^2\dot\theta_1^2$$

For $m_2$ — expand the binomials, then group:

$$\begin{aligned} v_2^2 = & \, L_1^2\dot\theta_1^2\big(\sin^2\theta_1+\cos^2\theta_1\big) \\ & + L_2^2(\dot\theta_1+\dot\theta_2)^2\big(\sin^2(\theta_1+\theta_2)+\cos^2(\theta_1+\theta_2)\big) \\ & + 2L_1L_2\dot\theta_1(\dot\theta_1+\dot\theta_2)\big[\sin\theta_1\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2) + \cos\theta_1\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2)\big] \end{aligned}$$

The square-bracketed factor is exactly the angle-difference identity $\cos(\alpha-\beta) = \cos\alpha\cos\beta + \sin\alpha\sin\beta$ with $\alpha = \theta_1+\theta_2$ and $\beta = \theta_1$, which collapses to $\cos\theta_2$. So:

$$\boxed{\, v_2^2 = L_1^2\dot\theta_1^2 + L_2^2(\dot\theta_1+\dot\theta_2)^2 + 2L_1L_2\cos\theta_2\,\dot\theta_1(\dot\theta_1+\dot\theta_2) \,}$$

Identity used. $\cos(\alpha-\beta) = \cos\alpha\cos\beta + \sin\alpha\sin\beta$. With $\alpha=\theta_1+\theta_2$, $\beta=\theta_1$, the difference $\alpha-\beta = \theta_2$.
Sanity check. Set $\theta_2 = 0$ — the arm is fully extended. Then $v_2^2 = (L_1+L_2)^2\dot\theta_1^2$ when $\dot\theta_2 = 0$, which is exactly what a single rigid link of length $L_1+L_2$ should give.
The $\cos\theta_2$ that pops out is the inertial coupling between the two joints. Remember it — that single cosine is the reason your robot 'feels' link 2's motion at joint 1.

5Step 5 — Total kinetic energy $T$

With both squared speeds in hand, the total kinetic energy is the sum of two simple $\tfrac12 m v^2$ terms:

$$T = \tfrac12 m_1 v_1^2 + \tfrac12 m_2 v_2^2$$

Substituting the explicit expressions:

$$\boxed{\, T = \tfrac12 m_1 L_1^2\dot\theta_1^2 + \tfrac12 m_2 \Big[ L_1^2\dot\theta_1^2 + L_2^2(\dot\theta_1+\dot\theta_2)^2 + 2L_1L_2\cos\theta_2\,\dot\theta_1(\dot\theta_1+\dot\theta_2) \Big] \,}$$

This is a quadratic form in the joint rates $\dot\theta_1, \dot\theta_2$ with configuration-dependent coefficients — every robot dynamics result you will see has this same shape: $T = \tfrac12 \dot{q}^T M(q) \dot{q}$.

The mass matrix is hiding here. Pulling out coefficients of $\dot\theta_1^2$, $\dot\theta_2^2$, and $\dot\theta_1\dot\theta_2$ gives the inertia matrix $M(q)$ directly. We will do this explicitly next module.
If you squint, the bracketed expression for $m_2$ is already the mass-matrix entries $M_{11}, M_{12}, M_{22}$ in disguise. Module 3 just makes them explicit.

6Step 6 — Potential energy $V$

Take $y$ positive upward and let gravity $g$ act in $-y$. The gravitational potential of each point mass is $mgh$, where $h$ is its height above the base:

$$V = m_1 g\, y_1 + m_2 g\, y_2$$

Substitute the positions found in Step 2:

$$\boxed{\, V = m_1 g L_1\sin\theta_1 + m_2 g\big[L_1\sin\theta_1 + L_2\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2)\big] \,}$$

Notice that $V$ depends only on the configuration $\theta_1, \theta_2$ — never on the rates. That is what will make the gravity term separate cleanly in the Euler–Lagrange equations.

Datum choice. We measured heights from the base joint (the origin of $T_1$). Any other horizontal datum would only shift $V$ by a constant, which Euler–Lagrange happily ignores.

7Step 7 — Assemble the Lagrangian $L = T - V$

Subtract the potential from the kinetic energy. Writing it out fully:

$$\begin{aligned} L \;=\;& \tfrac12 m_1 L_1^2\dot\theta_1^2 \\ &+ \tfrac12 m_2 \Big[ L_1^2\dot\theta_1^2 + L_2^2(\dot\theta_1+\dot\theta_2)^2 + 2L_1L_2\cos\theta_2\,\dot\theta_1(\dot\theta_1+\dot\theta_2) \Big] \\ &- m_1 g L_1\sin\theta_1 \\ &- m_2 g \big[L_1\sin\theta_1 + L_2\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2)\big] \end{aligned}$$

This single scalar function $L(\theta_1, \theta_2, \dot\theta_1, \dot\theta_2)$ now contains everything about the dynamics of the two-link arm. In Module 3 we will feed it to the Euler–Lagrange equations

$$\frac{d}{dt}\!\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot\theta_i}\right) - \frac{\partial L}{\partial \theta_i} = \tau_i$$

and recover the full equations of motion — including the inertia matrix, Coriolis terms, and gravity vector — without ever writing a Newton equation again.

What you have just built. A reusable template. Identical workflow handles a 3-link arm, a planar SCARA, even an underactuated cart-pole — only the geometry of Step 1 changes.
This is the payoff. Every line we wrote was in service of this $L$.
Workflow as pseudocode
1.  Write T_i for each link  (homogeneous transforms)
2.  Read tip positions       (last column of each T_i)
3.  Differentiate -> velocities (chain rule, watch summed angles)
4.  Form v_i^2 and simplify  (sin^2+cos^2 = 1, cos(a-b) identity)
5.  Build T = sum (1/2) m_i v_i^2
6.  Build V = sum m_i g y_i
7.  L = T - V
   --> hand to Euler-Lagrange in Module 3

8Wrap-up

We took a 2-link planar arm with tip masses and walked, step by step, from homogeneous transformation matrices all the way to a closed-form Lagrangian. Along the way we picked up two patterns worth memorizing: the angle-difference identity collapsing the cross-term to $\cos\theta_2$, and the kinetic energy emerging as a quadratic form $\tfrac12 \dot q^T M(q)\dot q$.

Next module: feed this $L$ into the Euler–Lagrange equations and read off the equations of motion — inertia, Coriolis/centripetal, and gravity terms all at once.

Bridge to Module 3. Apply $\frac{d}{dt}\!\left(\partial L/\partial \dot\theta_i\right) - \partial L/\partial \theta_i = \tau_i$ to extract $M(q)\ddot q + C(q,\dot q)\dot q + g(q) = \tau$.
If today felt mechanical, that is the point. Lagrangian dynamics is engineering by checklist — and you now have the checklist.

★Homework

Three exercises to lock the seven-step workflow into reflex. Show working — the algebra is the lesson.

1 · Reproduce the derivation

Working only from the schematic of the 2-link planar arm with tip masses, reproduce all seven steps end-to-end without consulting the lecture notes. Hand in $T_1$, $T_2$, the position equations, the velocities, $v_1^2$ and $v_2^2$, $T$, $V$, and $L$.

2 · Identify the mass matrix

Starting from the kinetic energy $T$ derived in class, write it explicitly in the form $T = \tfrac12 \dot q^T M(q)\dot q$ with $q = (\theta_1, \theta_2)^T$. Read off the entries $M_{11}, M_{12}=M_{21}, M_{22}$ as functions of $\theta_2$ and the link parameters.

3 · Sanity-check limits

Verify the Lagrangian behaves sensibly in two limits: (a) $L_2 \to 0$ should reduce the system to a single pendulum of mass $m_1+m_2$ at radius $L_1$; (b) $\theta_2 = 0$ with $\dot\theta_2=0$ should give $v_2^2 = (L_1+L_2)^2\dot\theta_1^2$. Show both algebraically.
