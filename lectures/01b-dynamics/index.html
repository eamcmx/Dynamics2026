Introduction to Robot Dynamics

From motion description to force analysis — why kinematics alone isn't enough.

Lecturer. bibliosense Lecture. 01
You already know how to describe where a robot is and how its links move — that's kinematics. Today we take the step that makes everything else in this course possible: we ask what forces and torques actually produce that motion. We'll see why a kinematically valid trajectory can still be physically impossible, meet the equation $\boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})\ddot{\mathbf{q}} + \mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}}) + \mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q})$ that will follow us for the rest of the module, and split the world into forward and inverse dynamics problems.

0What this lecture covers

This first lecture sets the stage for the whole dynamics course. We start from where kinematics leaves off and motivate why we need a deeper layer of analysis. Then we introduce the canonical form of the robot equation of motion, separate it into its three physically meaningful pieces (inertia, velocity-coupling, gravity), and distinguish the two complementary problem classes — forward and inverse dynamics — that we will keep coming back to.

By the end you should be able to answer three questions: what does kinematics not tell us? what does the equation $\boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})\ddot{\mathbf{q}} + \mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}}) + \mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q})$ encode? and when do I solve it forward versus inverse?

Think of this lecture as the bridge: kinematics was the map, dynamics is the terrain.
Learning objectives. Understand the kinematics→dynamics transition, the role of forces and torques, the forward vs. inverse split, and the mathematical framework we'll build on throughout the module.

1From kinematics to dynamics

Kinematics gave you a complete geometric description of motion: forward kinematics for end-effector pose, inverse kinematics for joint configurations, the Jacobian for velocities, its derivative for accelerations. That's a powerful toolkit, but it is silent on one thing — cause. Kinematics tells you what moves and how it moves relative to other parts. It cannot tell you how much torque a motor must deliver, why the arm overshoots its target on a fast move, or how a heavy payload changes the motor sizing.

Dynamics answers the next question: what forces and torques produce this motion? It is the layer where mass, inertia, gravity, and friction enter the picture, and where the clean linear-algebra world of kinematics gives way to coupled nonlinear differential equations. Every dynamic analysis still begins with kinematic quantities — positions, velocities, accelerations — so dynamics doesn't replace kinematics, it sits on top of it.

Kinematics is the map from A to B. Dynamics is the fuel, the engine, and the hill in the middle.
Why the jump in difficulty. Kinematics is geometric and largely linear. Dynamics adds physical properties — mass, inertia, friction — that couple the joints and make the equations nonlinear and configuration-dependent.

2Why dynamic modelling matters

Dynamic modelling is what separates a robot that works in a lab demo from one that performs reliably on a production line. Without a dynamic model you cannot size actuators correctly, you cannot design a controller that holds precision at speed, and you cannot predict energy use or vibration.

The physics behind this is Newton's second law, but applied to a multi-link manipulator it explodes into a system of coupled nonlinear ODEs. Four effects drive that complexity:

  • Inertial coupling — accelerating one joint accelerates every link distal to it; the effective inertia at a joint depends on the whole posture.
  • Gravitational loading — each link's weight produces a torque that depends on orientation; a horizontal link loads the joint, a vertical one barely does.
  • Velocity-dependent forces — Coriolis and centrifugal terms appear whenever multiple joints move together, and at high speed they can consume a large fraction of actuator capacity.
  • Configuration dependency — the inertia seen by an actuator changes dramatically with posture, so a controller tuned in one pose can fail in another.

This is why naive approaches — "just turn up the gains" — break down as soon as speed and precision matter.

A controller without a dynamic model is a controller fighting physics it doesn't understand.
Newton's law, robot edition. $\mathbf{F}=m\mathbf{a}$ is still true — but for an n-link manipulator it becomes n coupled nonlinear ODEs whose coefficients depend on configuration.
Where it bites in practice. Precision manufacturing (vibration, surface finish), medical robotics (force feedback, safety), and any high-speed pick-and-place all live or die by their dynamic model.

3Case study — automotive welding robot

Consider a 6-DOF arm welding car body panels at up to 1.5 m/s with ±0.1 mm accuracy. This single application exercises every piece of the dynamic equation we are about to introduce.

Inertia compensation. Rapid acceleration from rest creates large inertial forces that pure position feedback cannot react to in time. A feedforward term computed from $\boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})\ddot{\mathbf{q}} + \mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}}) + \mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q})$ predicts and cancels them.

Gravity compensation. Extended horizontally, the arm sags under its own weight by several millimetres — far more than the ±0.1 mm tolerance. Real-time evaluation of $\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q})$ makes the arm appear weightless to the position loop.

Velocity-dependent compensation. Curved paths at high speed generate Coriolis and centrifugal forces that can reach 20–30% of actuator capacity. The $\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}})$ term cancels them.

The quantitative payoff: accuracy from ±0.5 mm to ±0.05 mm (10×), 40% faster cycle time, 25% lower energy use, and an 85% drop in scrap. The dynamic model paid for itself in six months.

Same robot, same path, same payload — adding the dynamic model alone is the difference between scrap and a clean weld.
Concrete payoff. 10× accuracy improvement, +40% throughput, −25% energy, −85% scrap. Dynamic compensation isn't decoration — it's the line between viable and not.

4Forward vs. inverse dynamics

All of robot dynamics splits cleanly into two complementary problems, both built on the same equation but solved in opposite directions.

Forward dynamics — given the torques, find the motion. If I push this hard, where does the robot go? This is what a simulator does. Given $\boldsymbol{\tau}(t)$ you solve $\ddot{\mathbf{q}} = \mathbf{D}^{-1}(\mathbf{q})[\boldsymbol{\tau} - \mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}}) - \mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q})]$ and integrate. It is the more computationally expensive direction because of the matrix inversion, and its main use is simulation, prediction, and verification.

Inverse dynamics — given the motion, find the torques. If I want the robot on this trajectory, what do I send to the motors? Given a desired $\mathbf{q}(t),\dot{\mathbf{q}}(t),\ddot{\mathbf{q}}(t)$ you compute $\boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})\ddot{\mathbf{q}} + \mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}}) + \mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q})$ directly. It is cheaper and is the workhorse of real-time control and actuator sizing.

Forward dynamics predicts. Inverse dynamics plans. Real systems need both.
Surgical robot uses both. Pre-operative planning uses inverse dynamics to compute feedforward torques for a planned trajectory; during surgery, forward dynamics runs in parallel to predict motion and detect anomalous contact in real time.
Same equation, two directions. Both problems start from $\boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})\ddot{\mathbf{q}} + \mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}}) + \mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q})$. Forward solves for $\ddot{\mathbf{q}}$, inverse solves for $\boldsymbol{\tau}$.
forward dynamics — simulation
given:  τ(t)
find:   q̈(t) = D(q)⁻¹ [ τ − h(q,q̇) − c(q) ]
then integrate to get q̇(t), q(t)
inverse dynamics — control
given:  q(t), q̇(t), q̈(t)
find:   τ(t) = D(q) q̈ + h(q,q̇) + c(q)

5The fundamental equation and its three pieces

All of rigid-body manipulator dynamics, in its standard form, condenses into one line:

\[ \boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})\ddot{\mathbf{q}} + \mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}}) + \mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q}) \tag{1.1} \]

For an $n$-DOF manipulator each symbol has a clear physical meaning:

  • $\boldsymbol{\tau}$ — $n\times 1$ vector of joint torques (or forces, for prismatic joints).
  • $\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})$ — the $n\times n$ inertia matrix, symmetric and positive-definite. Diagonal entries are the effective inertia at each joint; off-diagonal entries encode inertial coupling between joints. Crucially, $\mathbf{D}$ depends on configuration $\mathbf{q}$.
  • $\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}})$ — $n\times 1$ vector of Coriolis and centrifugal terms. These are the velocity-dependent interaction forces; they vanish at zero velocity and grow quadratically with speed.
  • $\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q})$ — $n\times 1$ vector of gravitational torques. Position-dependent only, derivable from a potential energy.

Reading the equation physically: the torque you apply at the joints has to (a) accelerate the structure through $\mathbf{D}\ddot{\mathbf{q}}$, (b) overcome the velocity-induced cross-coupling $\mathbf{h}$, and (c) hold up against gravity $\mathbf{c}$.

Memorise the three buckets — inertia, velocity, gravity — and the rest of the course is bookkeeping.
D(q) — inertia. $n\times n$, symmetric, positive-definite, configuration-dependent. Off-diagonals are coupling between joints.
h(q,q̇) — Coriolis & centrifugal. $n\times 1$, vanishes at rest, scales quadratically with velocity: $\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\alpha\dot{\mathbf{q}}) = \alpha^2\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}})$.
c(q) — gravity. $n\times 1$, depends only on configuration, derivable from a potential. Bounded for any finite pose.
equation of motion
τ = D(q) q̈ + h(q, q̇) + c(q)

τ : n×1 joint torques
D : n×n inertia matrix     (sym, pos-def)
h : n×1 Coriolis + centrifugal
c : n×1 gravity

Now you derive the torque yourself. One bucket at a time — inertia, velocity, gravity. By the third configuration you'll know which term is going to dominate before you compute it.

6Mathematical properties we will rely on

The structure of equation (1.1) is not arbitrary — it carries a few properties that we will exploit again and again when we design controllers and prove stability.

Symmetry & positive-definiteness of $\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})$.

\[ \mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q}) = \mathbf{D}^T(\mathbf{q}),\qquad \mathbf{x}^T\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})\mathbf{x} > 0\ \forall\ \mathbf{x}\neq\mathbf{0} \tag{1.2} \]

This is what guarantees the kinetic energy $\tfrac12\dot{\mathbf{q}}^T\mathbf{D}\dot{\mathbf{q}}$ is positive and that $\mathbf{D}$ is invertible — both essential for forward dynamics and for energy arguments.

Skew-symmetry property. With $\mathbf{C}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}})$ a particular Coriolis matrix factorisation,

\[ \dot{\mathbf{D}}(\mathbf{q}) - 2\mathbf{C}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}})\ \text{is skew-symmetric.} \tag{1.3} \]

This encodes passivity — the manipulator does not generate energy out of nowhere — and is the workhorse of nonlinear robot control proofs.

Boundedness. $\mathbf{D}$ is bounded above and below over the workspace, $\mathbf{h}$ is bounded by a quadratic in $\dot{\mathbf{q}}$, and $\mathbf{c}$ is bounded for any finite configuration. These bounds let us reason about worst-case torques and design robust controllers.

Symmetry, positive-definiteness, passivity. Three words that make every controllability proof in this module possible.
Why passivity matters. Passivity is energy-honesty: the system cannot produce more mechanical energy than is supplied. It is the cleanest hook we have for proving stability of nonlinear robot controllers.

The inertia matrix isn't a black box — for a 2-DOF arm you can write it down on a napkin. Build it, then we'll check the two properties that make every stability proof in this course possible.

7How dynamics plugs into control

Dynamic models are not just descriptive — they are an active component of every modern robot controller. The standard architecture has the inverse dynamics doing the heavy lifting and a feedback loop cleaning up what's left:

  1. Trajectory planner generates the desired motion $\mathbf{q}_d(t),\dot{\mathbf{q}}_d(t),\ddot{\mathbf{q}}_d(t)$.
  2. Inverse dynamics (feedforward) computes $\boldsymbol{\tau}_{ff} = \mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})\ddot{\mathbf{q}}_d + \mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}}) + \mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q})$.
  3. Feedback controller produces a corrective $\boldsymbol{\tau}_{fb}$ from the tracking error.
  4. Total torque sent to the actuators: $\boldsymbol{\tau}_{total} = \boldsymbol{\tau}_{ff} + \boldsymbol{\tau}_{fb}$.
  5. Forward dynamics (the real plant) turns that torque into actual motion.

The key idea: the better your model, the smaller the error the feedback loop has to correct, and the better the achievable performance. A perfect model needs no feedback; a missing model forces feedback to fight physics.

Feedforward does the planning, feedback does the apologising. Get the model right and there's less to apologise for.
The control split. $\boldsymbol{\tau}_{total} = \boldsymbol{\tau}_{ff} + \boldsymbol{\tau}_{fb}$ — feedforward from inverse dynamics, feedback from tracking error.

q_d, q̇_d, q̈_d  ──▶  inverse dynamics ──▶ τ_ff
                                            +
  q, q̇  ──▶  feedback controller ──▶ τ_fb

8Prerequisites and what comes next

Before the next lecture, make sure you are comfortable with:

  • Kinematics — transformation matrices, Jacobians, velocity/acceleration relationships.
  • Mathematics — matrix algebra, multivariable calculus, ordinary differential equations.
  • Physics — Newton's laws, energy conservation, momentum.
  • Programming — basic numerical computation in MATLAB or Python.

The module will then proceed in three steps. Module 2 introduces the Lagrangian / energy-based formulation that gives us a systematic way to derive equation (1.1) for any manipulator.
