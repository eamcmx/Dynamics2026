Lecture 07 · Module 1 · Linear Algebra & Vector Geometry

Scalar product & the angle between vectors

Ten worked problems on dot products, cosines, orthogonality, and parallel vectors in 2D and 3D.

Lecturer. bibliosense Lecture. 07
Today is a workshop, not a lecture. We have one tool — the scalar product — and we are going to use it ten times in a row, on vectors in the plane and in space, until the geometry is automatic. Every problem is a variation of the same loop: compute $\vec{A}\cdot\vec{B}$, divide by the product of magnitudes, read off the angle. By the end you should know, just by looking, whether two vectors are orthogonal, parallel, or somewhere in between.

0What this lecture covers

This problem set drills the scalar (dot) product and the angle between vectors in $\mathbb{R}^2$ and $\mathbb{R}^3$. Every problem reduces to one of three questions: what is $\vec{A}\cdot\vec{B}$?, what is the cosine of the angle between them?, or what is the geometric relationship — orthogonal, parallel, or oblique?

We work through ten problems in order. The first few are pure computation; later ones use the sign of the dot product and the value of $\cos\theta$ to classify the geometric relationship between the two vectors.

Don't memorise ten answers — memorise the one formula and run it ten times.
Core formulas.$\vec{A}\cdot\vec{B}=\sum_i A_iB_i$ and $\cos\theta=\dfrac{\vec{A}\cdot\vec{B}}{\lVert\vec{A}\rVert\,\lVert\vec{B}\rVert}$.
Sign tells you geometry.Positive dot product → acute angle. Zero → orthogonal. Negative → obtuse. $\cos\theta=\pm 1$ → parallel.

1Problem 1 — Plain dot product in 2D

Let $\vec{A}=(3,4)$ and $\vec{B}=(5,2)$. Find $\vec{A}\cdot\vec{B}$.

This is the warm-up: just multiply matching components and sum. No magnitudes, no cosines.

Component-wise dot product
A·B = 3·5 + 4·2
    = 15 + 8
    = 23

2Problem 2 — Cosine of the angle in 2D

Given $\vec{A}=(-2,6)$ and $\vec{B}=(4,-3)$, compute $\cos\theta$.

Three quantities are needed: $\vec{A}\cdot\vec{B}$, $\lVert\vec{A}\rVert$, and $\lVert\vec{B}\rVert$. Plug into the cosine formula. The negative result tells you the angle is obtuse.

Read the sign first.Before reaching for a calculator, note that $\vec{A}\cdot\vec{B}<0$ — so $\theta>90°$ is already guaranteed.
Plug into the cosine formula
A·B   = (-2)(4) + (6)(-3) = -8 - 18 = -26
||A|| = sqrt(4 + 36)  = sqrt(40)
||B|| = sqrt(16 + 9)  = sqrt(25) = 5

cos θ = -26 / (5·sqrt(40)) ≈ -0.822

3Problem 3 — Angle in degrees

For $\vec{A}=(7,-1)$ and $\vec{B}=(-3,5)$, find both $\vec{A}\cdot\vec{B}$ and the angle in degrees.

Same loop as Problem 2, but now we go one step further and apply $\arccos$. Watch the units — the answer is asked for in degrees, not radians.

From cosine to degrees
A·B   = (7)(-3) + (-1)(5) = -21 - 5 = -26
||A|| = sqrt(49 + 1)  = sqrt(50)
||B|| = sqrt(9 + 25)  = sqrt(34)

cos θ = -26 / sqrt(50·34) ≈ -0.6306
θ     = arccos(-0.6306) ≈ 129.1°

4Problem 4 — Orthogonality test in 3D

Let $\vec{A}=(2,-1,3)$ and $\vec{B}=(1,4,-2)$. Compute $\vec{A}\cdot\vec{B}$ and decide whether the vectors are orthogonal.

The formula extends to three dimensions by adding the $z$-component term. Two vectors are orthogonal iff their dot product is zero — so the test reduces to a single arithmetic check.

Orthogonality is the cheapest geometric question you can ask of two vectors: one number, one comparison.
Result.$\vec{A}\cdot\vec{B}=2-4-6=-8\neq 0$ — not orthogonal.
Three-component dot product
A·B = (2)(1) + (-1)(4) + (3)(-2)
    = 2 - 4 - 6
    = -8        ≠ 0  →  not orthogonal

5Problem 5 — Cosine in 3D

Given $\vec{A}=(3,4,0)$ and $\vec{B}=(0,-4,3)$, find $\cos\theta$.

Note that both vectors have magnitude 5 — that simplifies the denominator a lot. The dot product is dominated by the $-16$ term from the matched $y$-components.

Equal-magnitude shortcut
A·B   = (3)(0) + (4)(-4) + (0)(3) = -16
||A|| = sqrt(9 + 16 + 0) = 5
||B|| = sqrt(0 + 16 + 9) = 5

cos θ = -16 / (5·5) = -16/25 = -0.64

6Problem 6 — Angle in radians

For $\vec{A}=(1,2,2)$ and $\vec{B}=(3,0,1)$, compute $\vec{A}\cdot\vec{B}$ and find the angle in radians.

Same procedure as Problem 3, but the final $\arccos$ is left in radians. Useful reminder that radians, not degrees, are the natural unit when angles feed into further calculus.

Angle in radians
A·B   = (1)(3) + (2)(0) + (2)(1) = 5
||A|| = sqrt(1 + 4 + 4)  = 3
||B|| = sqrt(9 + 0 + 1)  = sqrt(10)

cos θ = 5 / (3·sqrt(10)) ≈ 0.527
θ     = arccos(0.527) ≈ 1.017 rad

7Problem 7 — When the cosine is exactly zero

Given $\vec{A}=(5,0,0)$ and $\vec{B}=(0,3,4)$, find $\cos\theta$ and comment on the geometry.

The dot product is zero — every term involves a factor of zero. So $\cos\theta=0$ and $\theta=90°$: the vectors are perpendicular. Geometrically, $\vec{A}$ points along the $x$-axis while $\vec{B}$ lies entirely in the $yz$-plane.

Geometric reading.$\vec{A}$ lies on the $x$-axis. $\vec{B}$ lives in the $yz$-plane. They meet only at the origin and at a right angle.
Orthogonal pair
A·B   = (5)(0) + (0)(3) + (0)(4) = 0
cos θ = 0       →     θ = π/2 = 90°

8Problem 8 — Negative components, obtuse angle

For $\vec{A}=(-3,-4)$ and $\vec{B}=(2,5)$, calculate $\vec{A}\cdot\vec{B}$ and the cosine of the angle between them.

Both components of $\vec{A}$ are negative; both components of $\vec{B}$ are positive — geometrically the vectors point into roughly opposite quadrants, so we expect $\cos\theta<0$.

Sign-aware computation
A·B   = (-3)(2) + (-4)(5) = -6 - 20 = -26
||A|| = sqrt(9 + 16)  = 5
||B|| = sqrt(4 + 25)  = sqrt(29)

cos θ = -26 / (5·sqrt(29)) ≈ -0.966

9Problem 9 — Detecting parallel vectors

For $\vec{A}=(1,-1,1)$ and $\vec{B}=(-2,2,-2)$, decide whether the vectors are parallel by computing $\vec{A}\cdot\vec{B}$ and analysing the angle.

Notice $\vec{B}=-2\,\vec{A}$ — every component of $\vec{B}$ is exactly $-2$ times the matching component of $\vec{A}$. So the vectors are anti-parallel: parallel lines, opposite directions, $\cos\theta=-1$, and $\theta=180°$.

Parallel doesn't mean 'pointing the same way' — it means 'on the same line'. The dot-product test gives you both flavours.
Two parallel signatures.$\cos\theta=+1$ → same direction. $\cos\theta=-1$ → opposite direction. Both count as parallel.
Anti-parallel pair
A·B   = (1)(-2) + (-1)(2) + (1)(-2) = -2 - 2 - 2 = -6
||A|| = sqrt(3)
||B|| = sqrt(12) = 2·sqrt(3)

cos θ = -6 / (sqrt(3)·2·sqrt(3)) = -6/6 = -1
θ     = 180°    →   anti-parallel

10Problem 10 — Full computation in 3D

Let $\vec{A}=(6,8,-3)$ and $\vec{B}=(-4,5,2)$. Find $\vec{A}\cdot\vec{B}$ and the angle between the two vectors.

A closing problem with no special structure — neither orthogonal nor parallel, mixed signs, three dimensions. Run the standard recipe end to end.

End-to-end recipe
A·B   = (6)(-4) + (8)(5) + (-3)(2)
      = -24 + 40 - 6 = 10
||A|| = sqrt(36 + 64 + 9)  = sqrt(109)
||B|| = sqrt(16 + 25 + 4)  = sqrt(45)

cos θ = 10 / sqrt(109·45) ≈ 0.1428
θ     = arccos(0.1428) ≈ 81.8°

11Pattern recap — three questions, one formula

Across all ten problems, only one formula did real work: $\cos\theta=\dfrac{\vec{A}\cdot\vec{B}}{\lVert\vec{A}\rVert\,\lVert\vec{B}\rVert}$. What changed was the question being asked of it.

  • Magnitude question — "what is $\vec{A}\cdot\vec{B}$?" (Problems 1, 4, parts of others).
  • Angle question — "what is $\theta$?" (Problems 2, 3, 5, 6, 8, 10).
  • Geometric-classification question — "are they orthogonal? parallel?" (Problems 4, 7, 9).

If you can map any new exercise onto one of those three buckets, you already know what to compute.

One formula, three lenses. Always ask: which lens does this problem want?
Quick classifier.$\vec{A}\cdot\vec{B}=0$ → orthogonal. $\cos\theta=\pm 1$ → parallel. Otherwise → oblique, and the sign of the dot product tells you acute vs. obtuse.
