Lecture 04 · Module 4 · Robot Dynamics and Control

Understanding each component of the dynamic model

From theory to practice — analysing the 5-DOF RV-M1 through its inertia, Coriolis and gravity terms.

Lecturer. bibliosense Lecture. 04
We've already written five_dof_dynamics.m and watched the equations spit out torques. Today we slow down and ask what each piece of $\tau = \mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})\ddot{\mathbf{q}} + \mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}}) + \mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q})$ is actually doing on the RV-M1 — which links dominate the inertia, when Coriolis stops being a footnote, and why gravity compensation is the single biggest win you can hand a PD loop. By the end you should be reading the code physically, not symbolically.

0What this lecture covers

Building on the RV-M1 case study and the five_dof_dynamics.m implementation, we dissect the three pieces of the manipulator equation one at a time. We'll look at the inertia matrix $\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})$ and how its entries change with configuration, the velocity-dependent vector $\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}})$ that captures Coriolis and centrifugal effects, and the gravity vector $\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q})$ that dominates static loading. We then connect each term back to lines of MATLAB code, explore optimisation opportunities, and end with a progression of controllers from naive PD up to full dynamic compensation.

Think of this as the autopsy lecture — same equations, but now we know which organ does what.
Learning targets.Inertia matrix analysis, velocity-dependent forces, gravitational loading, code↔maths connection, control-design implications, and computational strategy.

1The inertia matrix $\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})$ — configuration-dependent mass

In five_dof_dynamics.m you assembled a 5×5 symmetric matrix from trace(U*J*U') terms. Those entries aren't abstractions — they're the real coupling between the RV-M1's joints. The diagonal entries are self-inertias: $D_{11}$ is the inertia seen by the base when the whole arm rotates with it (largest when the arm is extended horizontally), $D_{22}$ is dominated by the 250 mm upper arm and everything outboard of it, $D_{33}$ by the 160 mm forearm and wrist, and $D_{44}, D_{55}$ are small wrist contributions.

The off-diagonal entries are coupling inertias. $D_{12}$ tells you how an acceleration of the base shows up as a torque demand at the shoulder; $D_{23}$ couples shoulder and elbow strongly because the forearm sits between them; $D_{13}$ becomes large when the arm is extended because the moment arms are long. Crucially, every one of these entries is a function of $\mathbf{q}$ — the matrix you compute at one pose is not the matrix you compute at another.

If you remember one thing: $\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})$ is not a constant. The robot you're controlling changes shape every millisecond.
Diagonal vs off-diagonal.Diagonals say how hard is it to accelerate this joint alone. Off-diagonals say what does accelerating this joint do to its neighbours.
Properties guaranteed by the formulation.Symmetry ($D_{ij}=D_{ji}$) and positive-definiteness fall out of the $\operatorname{tr}(U J U^{\top})$ construction — you don't have to enforce them by hand.
Compare the inertia matrix at three poses
% Compare inertia matrix for different configurations
config1 = [0; 0; 0; 0; 0];      % All joints at zero (reference)

2Coriolis & centrifugal terms $\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}})$

The Christoffel symbols h111, h112, … in your code assemble into the velocity-dependent vector $\mathbf{h}$. These are the phantom forces — they don't exist when the robot is still, but at high speed they can dominate. Centrifugal terms scale as $\dot{q}_i^2$ and arise from a single joint's motion (spin the base fast and the upper arm tries to fly outward). Coriolis terms scale as $\dot{q}_i\dot{q}_j$ and arise from interaction — moving joints 1 and 2 together produces a force at joint 3 that neither motion alone would produce.

For the RV-M1, the strongest Coriolis coupling comes from the shoulder–elbow pair through the 160 mm forearm. The practical rule of thumb: if your trajectory has any joint moving above ~3 rad/s, $\mathbf{h}$ is no longer optional in the controller.

Centrifugal goes as $\dot{q}^2$, Coriolis as $\dot{q}_i\dot{q}_j$. Doubling the speed quadruples the headache.
Phantom but predictable.These forces are tracking-error generators if you ignore them — but the model knows them exactly, so feedforward compensation is cheap.
Pure centrifugal — sweep speed at fixed pose
config = [0; 45; 30; 0; 0];  % Fixed configuration
acc = zeros(5,1);            % No acceleration

speeds = [0, 1, 2, 5, 10];   % rad/s
for i = 1:length(speeds)
    veloc = [speeds(i); 0; 0; 0; 0];
    torque = five_dof_dynamics(config, veloc, acc, 'deg');
    fprintf('Speed %.1f rad/s: Torque magnitude = %.3f N*m\n', ...
            speeds(i), norm(torque));
end
Isolating Coriolis interaction
config = [0; 45; 30; 0; 0];
acc = zeros(5,1);

veloc_single = [2; 0; 0; 0; 0];     % Only joint 1
veloc_both   = [2; 2; 0; 0; 0];     % Joints 1 and 2

torque_single = five_dof_dynamics(config, veloc_single, acc, 'deg');
torque_both   = five_dof_dynamics(config, veloc_both,   acc, 'deg');
torque_interaction = torque_both - torque_single;

fprintf('Interaction forces: %.3f N*m\n', norm(torque_interaction));

3When velocity forces actually matter

Not every controller needs the full $\mathbf{h}$ term. The RV-M1's geometry gives reasonably crisp speed bands where each modelling decision pays off, and these are the numbers worth keeping in your head when you're choosing between simple PID, gravity-compensated PD, and full dynamic feedforward.

Pick your controller for the speed band you actually operate in — not the one that sounds most rigorous.
Below 0.5 rad/s.Velocity terms are <1% of the torque budget. Plain PID with integral action is fine.
3–8 rad/s.15–40% of total torque is velocity-dependent. You need explicit dynamic compensation or your tracking falls apart.
Above 8 rad/s.Dominant velocity forces. Without the full model you're not really controlling the robot — you're hoping.

4Gravitational terms $\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q})$ — the static load

The cc vector in five_dof_dynamics.m carries the gravity loading. For the RV-M1 it's almost always the largest term in the static torque budget, and unlike $\mathbf{h}$ it's purely a function of pose — which makes it the easiest piece to feedforward.

Joint 1 (base, vertical axis) sees essentially zero gravity torque. Joint 2 (shoulder) sees the worst of it because it carries the entire 250 mm + 160 mm + wrist chain. Joint 3 carries less, the wrist joints almost nothing. As you sweep the shoulder angle from $-90°$ through $0°$ to $+90°$, the joint-2 gravity torque traces a clean $\sin(\theta_2)$: maximum at horizontal, zero at vertical.

Gravity never moves and never sleeps. Pay it once with feedforward and your PD loop only has to worry about motion.
Joint-by-joint static load.$J_1$: ≈0. $J_2$: dominant. $J_3$: moderate. $J_4, J_5$: small. This ranking is a property of the geometry, not the trajectory.
Gravity torque vs shoulder angle
veloc = zeros(5,1); acc = zeros(5,1);  % Static case

shoulder_angles = -90:15:90;  % degrees
gravity_torques = zeros(length(shoulder_angles), 5);

for i = 1:length(shoulder_angles)
    config = [0; shoulder_angles(i); 0; 0; 0];
    torque = five_dof_dynamics(config, veloc, acc, 'deg');
    gravity_torques(i, :) = torque';
end

figure;
plot(shoulder_angles, gravity_torques(:, 2), 'b-', 'LineWidth', 2);
xlabel('Shoulder Angle (degrees)');

5A complete control system: PD + gravity + forward dynamics

To go from torque equations to a moving robot we need three pieces: a PD law that turns errors into corrective torque, a gravity-compensation feedforward that uses five_dof_dynamics with zero velocity and acceleration, and a way to extract $\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})$ so we can integrate $\ddot{\mathbf{q}} = \mathbf{D}^{-1}(\tau - \mathbf{c})$ forward in time.

The trick in get_inertia_matrix_fd is elegant: drive each joint with a unit acceleration in turn, subtract off the gravity-only torque, and the remaining vector is one column of $\mathbf{D}$. Five calls give you the whole matrix — without ever exposing it from inside the function.

Think of this as the smallest closed loop that does anything useful: PD + gravity FF, with forward dynamics doing the integration.
Why subtract tau_g after dividing by D?tau already includes the gravity-compensation term we added. Subtracting tau_g again leaves only the net torque available for accelerating the links — exactly what $\mathbf{D}\ddot{\mathbf{q}}$ should equal.
PD + gravity-compensation controller
function tau_total = simple_rvm1_controller(q_desired, q_current, qd_current, Kp, Kd)
if nargin < 4
    Kp = diag([50, 100, 80, 30, 20]);
end
if nargin < 5
    Kd = diag([5, 10, 8, 3, 2]);
end

pos_error = q_desired - q_current;
vel_error = -qd_current;

tau_pd      = Kp * pos_error + Kd * vel_error;
tau_gravity = five_dof_dynamics(q_current, zeros(5,1), zeros(5,1), 'rad');

tau_total = tau_pd + tau_gravity;
end
Extract D(q) by unit-acceleration probing
function D = get_inertia_matrix_fd(q)
n = length(q);
D = zeros(n,n);

for j = 1:n
    qd  = zeros(n,1);
    qdd = zeros(n,1);
    qdd(j) = 1;                         % unit acceleration at joint j
    tau   = five_dof_dynamics(q, qd, qdd, 'rad');
    tau_0 = five_dof_dynamics(q, zeros(n,1), zeros(n,1), 'rad');
    D(:,j) = tau - tau_0;               % jth column of D(q)
end
end
Joint-2 step response — full simulation
clear; clc;
dt = 0.01; T = 5; t = 0:dt:T; n_joints = 5;

q  = zeros(n_joints,1);
qd = zeros(n_joints,1);

q_desired = zeros(n_joints,1);
q_desired(2) = deg2rad(45);

Kp = diag([0, 6, 0, 0, 0]);
Kd = diag([0, 1, 0, 0, 0]);

q_history   = zeros(n_joints,length(t));
qd_history  = zeros(n_joints,length(t));
tau_history = zeros(n_joints,length(t));

for k = 1:length(t)
    pos_error = q_desired - q;
    vel_error = -qd;
    tau_pd = Kp * pos_error + Kd * vel_error;

    tau_g = five_dof_dynamics(q, zeros(n_joints,1), zeros(n_joints,1), 'rad');
    tau   = tau_pd + tau_g;

    D   = get_inertia_matrix_fd(q);
    qdd = D \ (tau - tau_g);

    qd = qd + qdd * dt;
    q  = q  + qd  * dt;

    q_history(:,k)   = q;
    qd_history(:,k)  = qd;
    tau_history(:,k) = tau;
end

6Hands-on experiments to run

The simulator only earns its keep if you probe it. Four experiments expose the most important behaviours: turning gravity compensation on and off (steady-state error tells you everything), sweeping gains from sluggish to oscillatory to find the sweet spot, driving multiple joints simultaneously to surface the coupling terms, and running the same trajectory from different initial poses to see how dramatically configuration changes the answer.

7Where five_dof_dynamics.m is slow — and what to do

The provided code is faithful and easy to read, but it predates modern symbolic toolchains. It computes 125 Christoffel symbols line-by-line, stores every $U$ matrix explicitly, and hardcodes link lengths and masses. The optimisation opportunities sit in three layers: vectorise the trace sums and exploit the symmetry $h_{ijk}=h_{ikj}$ to halve the work; reduce storage by computing $U$ matrices on demand or recursively; and generalise away from the RV-M1's specific dimensions.

For real-time use the right move is usually to switch algorithm entirely — recursive Newton-Euler is $O(n)$ versus the Lagrangian formulation's $O(n^4)$. For this course we keep the Lagrangian form because it makes the structure visible; in production you'd pre-compute symbolic expressions and emit C++.

This code was written to teach the equations, not to ship them. Optimising it is the rite of passage from student to engineer.
Algorithmic vs micro-optimisation.A 2× win from vectorising trace sums is nice. A 100× win from switching to recursive Newton-Euler is the real unlock.

8Component decomposition with one function

You don't need extra code to separate $\mathbf{D}\ddot{\mathbf{q}}$, $\mathbf{h}$, and $\mathbf{c}$ — clever calls to five_dof_dynamics do it for you. Call it with $(\mathbf{q}, 0, 0)$ to get pure gravity. Call it with $(\mathbf{q}, 0, \ddot{\mathbf{q}})$ and subtract gravity to get pure inertial torque. Call it with $(\mathbf{q}, \dot{\mathbf{q}}, 0)$ minus gravity to get the velocity-dependent piece. The percentages of total torque attributable to each component are the single best diagnostic for why a controller is failing.

Three calls, three components. Print the percentages and the failure mode usually announces itself.
What the percentages tell you.Joint dominated by gravity → invest in compensation. Joint dominated by velocity terms → you're operating in the dynamic regime. Joint dominated by inertial term → your trajectory is aggressive.
Decompose the torque into its three components
function analyze_dynamic_components(q, qd, qdd)
tau_total    = five_dof_dynamics(q, qd, qdd, 'rad');
tau_gravity  = five_dof_dynamics(q, zeros(5,1), zeros(5,1), 'rad');
tau_inertial = five_dof_dynamics(q, zeros(5,1), qdd, 'rad') - tau_gravity;
tau_velocity = five_dof_dynamics(q, qd, zeros(5,1), 'rad') - tau_gravity;

fprintf('                J1     J2     J3     J4     J5\n');
fprintf('Total:     %6.2f %6.2f %6.2f %6.2f %6.2f\n', tau_total);
fprintf('Gravity:   %6.2f %6.2f %6.2f %6.2f %6.2f\n', tau_gravity);
fprintf('Inertial:  %6.2f %6.2f %6.2f %6.2f %6.2f\n', tau_inertial);
fprintf('Velocity:  %6.2f %6.2f %6.2f %6.2f %6.2f\n', tau_velocity);
end

9Three controllers, in order of cost vs benefit

Treat controller design as a ladder. Level 1 is naive PD with no model — works at low speed, sluggish under gravity, the baseline you compare everything else to. Level 2 adds gravity compensation; for the RV-M1 this is almost free (one extra call to five_dof_dynamics per cycle) and removes nearly all the steady-state error. Level 3 is full dynamic compensation: pass desired $(\mathbf{q}, \dot{\mathbf{q}}, \ddot{\mathbf{q}})$ into the model as feedforward and let PD only handle deviations. Level 3 wins at trajectory tracking; for point-to-point moves Level 2 is usually the right engineering choice.

Don't pay for Level 3 if your task lives at Level 2 speeds. Match the controller to the regime.
Where each level wins.L1: prototyping. L2: positioning, pick-and-place, anything below ~3 rad/s. L3: trajectory tracking, high-speed work, contact tasks.
Level 3 — feedforward + feedback
function tau_full = full_dynamic_control(q_desired, qd_desired, qdd_desired, ...
                                         q_current, qd_current)
tau_feedforward = five_dof_dynamics(q_current, qd_current, qdd_desired, 'rad');

Kp = diag([50, 100, 80, 30, 20]);
Kd = diag([5,   10,  8,  3,  2]);

pos_error = q_desired  - q_current;
vel_error = qd_desired - qd_current;

tau_feedback = Kp * pos_error + Kd * vel_error;
tau_full = tau_feedforward + tau_feedback;
end

10Summary and where Module 5 takes us

By the end of this lecture you should be able to point at any line of five_dof_dynamics.m and say which physical effect it captures, predict whether velocity terms matter for a given trajectory before you simulate, and pick the cheapest controller that meets the task. The RV-M1's geometry — the 250 mm upper arm and the 160 mm forearm — is now part of your physical intuition: those two links dominate inertia, set the gravity load on joint 2, and create the strongest Coriolis coupling.

Module 5 will lift these ideas into stability and performance: Lyapunov analysis with the manipulator equation, linearisation around operating points, recursive Newton-Euler for real-time use, and parameter identification when the link masses you have on paper don't quite match the robot you have on the bench.
